No, you should not add regular silica sand to stretch your MIZZOU castable. Doing so will cause your intermediate backup layer to crack, crumble, and structurally fail under kiln temperatures.
While adding construction sand seems like a cheap way to increase volume, it introduces three destructive problems into a high-temperature refractory matrix:

Why Silica Sand Fails in a Kiln

• The Quartz Inversion (Structural Failure): Regular sand is crystalline silica. When heated to 1,063°F (573°C), silica undergoes a sudden, violent atomic rearrangement known as the quartz inversion. The sand grains instantly expand by about 1% to 2% and then contract upon cooling. This continuous movement will completely shatter the surrounding hydraulic cement bond, turning your solid backup layer back into loose, powdery sand after just a few firings.
• Chemical Fluxing (Melting): MIZZOU is engineered as a high-alumina refractory (60%+ alumina) to withstand extreme heat. Introducing free silica (sand) into a mix that contains alumina and calcium aluminate cement creates a eutectic reaction. At kiln temperatures, these three elements react to form low-melting-point glasses. Essentially, the sand acts as a flux, causing the internal structure of the refractory to melt and sag from the inside out.
• Thermal Conductivity (Zero Insulation): Sand is an incredibly efficient conductor of heat. Adding it to MIZZOU will make the backup layer denser and much hotter, completely defeating your goal of creating an energy-efficient, insulating layer to protect your kiln's outer shell.

Safe Materials to "Stretch" MIZZOU

The Chemistry: The Mullite Expansion

Almost all ceramic materials shrink as they fire because the clay particles melt slightly and pack closer together (sintering).
Kyanite (Al₂O₃ ⋅ SiO₂) is unique. When heated to around 2100°F–2400°F, it irreversibly decomposes into Mullite and amorphous silica. This chemical phase change forces the kyanite crystals to expand permanently by 3% to 5% in volume.
By adding a precise percentage of raw kyanite to your mix, its physical expansion perfectly counteracts the natural contraction of your Greenstripe fireclay. Because the kyanite replaces dense grog rather than your foam bubbles, your insulation value, maximum temperature rating, and fired strength remain completely unaffected.

The Adjusted K26 "Zero-Shrink" Formulation (By Weight)

Clay Art Center regularly stocks RM246 Kyanite Grog (35 Mesh). You will adjust your dry base to include 8% Kyanite by shaving a bit off your CE70 grog:
• 52% CE70 Mullite Grog (Down from 60%)
• 8% Raw Kyanite (35 mesh) (New expansion anchor)
• 30% Greenstripe Fireclay (Unchanged ceramic bond)
• 10% Pure Dextrin Powder (Unchanged green binder)

Physical Sizing Strategy

When buying the kyanite, ensure you stick with a coarse mesh size like 35 mesh or 48 mesh. Coarser kyanite grains behave like an interlocking "felt" matrix inside your cell walls, giving the foam higher structural stability while it fires. Avoid ultra-fine 100 or 325 mesh kyanite, as the high surface area will absorb too much of your sticky dextrin water and make your slip too thick to fold into the surfactant foam properly.

To achieve a high-performance low-slump pour with this specific foam-dextrin slurry, your target water addition is:
• 22% to 26% water by weight of the dry base mix.
While a standard hydraulic concrete or commercial castable relies on a ultra-low 5% to 8% water ratio, your starch consolidation casting (SCC) method behaves completely differently. Dissolved dextrin is a highly viscous, sticky polymer. If you drop below 22% water, the clay and CE70 grog will turn into an unworkable, dough-like mass that cannot trap or fold in the surfactant foam bubbles evenly.
Conversely, going above 26% creates a runny slip. A high-slump, watery mix reduces the viscosity of the bubble walls, leading to rapid bubble drainage, aggregate settling, and a collapsed, dense wall.

Achieving the Ideal "Low-Slump" Consistency

Because a slump cone test can prematurely pop the foam bubbles, judge the pour using a visual shear test during the mix:
1. The Dextrin Syrup Phase: Dissolve the 10 lbs of dextrin into the 24 lbs of water first. It should feel like a thick, highly tacky syrup.
2. The Slurry State: Once the clay, grog, and kyanite are fully mixed into the dextrin water, it should have the consistency of a heavy mortar or a thick peanut butter. It will require mechanical force (a paddle mixer) to move smoothly.
3. The Foam Fold: Fold in your whipped surfactant foam. The air bubbles will actually act like microscopic ball bearings, slightly improving the flow of the mix without adding any more liquid water.
4. The Target Pour Look: When poured into your 2" wall forms, the material should hold its shape and require gentle tapping or vibrating against the outside of the formwork to settle and fill the corners. If it flows completely flat on its own like pancake batter, it is too wet; immediately add a handful of dry-blended clay and grog to rescue the batch.

Here is the exact recipe and step-by-step procedure scaled down to a 50-lb dry batch, matching your low-slump, zero-shrinkage target.

Step-by-Step Batching & Mixing Protocol


1. Dry Blend the Refractories

In a clean 5-gallon or 7-gallon bucket, thoroughly blend your 26 lbs of CE70 grog, 15 lbs of Greenstripe clay, and 4 lbs of raw Kyanite. Use a hand scoop or mask up and run the paddle mixer on low to ensure the expansion-controlling kyanite is evenly distributed through the grog.

2. Create the Dextrin Glue

In your primary mixing bucket, pour 12 lbs of lukewarm water. Slowly add your 5 lbs of home-converted dextrin powder while running the paddle mixer. Dextrin dissolves beautifully in warm water. Mix until it forms a highly tacky, uniform syrup with zero dry lumps.

3. Build the Thick Slurry

With the paddle mixer running at medium speed, gradually dump your dry-blended refractory mix into the dextrin syrup.
• The Consistency: It will quickly become very heavy and look like thick, sticky peanut butter or stiff masonry mortar.
• The Low-Slump Check: If the paddle mixer strains but moves the mud smoothly, you are right in the sweet spot. If it is completely unmanageable for your drill, add up to an extra 1 lb of water max, but do it incrementally.

4. Whip and Fold the Dawn Foam

1. In a separate container, combine roughly 1.5 to 2 cups of water with 2 to 3 tablespoons of Dawn dish soap.
2. Use a clean kitchen hand-whisk or a separate clean mixing attachment to whip it into a dense, completely dry foam. You want micro-bubbles, not big soapy suds.
3. Dump the stiff foam directly onto your thick clay slurry. Do not use the power paddle mixer for this step. Use a wide rubber trowel, a hand scoop, or your hands to gently fold the foam into the mud. Stop folding the second the mix looks uniform and light—over-mixing will pop the bubbles and cause the castable to lose its insulation value.

5. The Low-Slump Pour

Pour the aerated mix directly into your oiled, cardboard-lined 2" forms. Because it is a low-slump mix, it will not flow like water. Use a trowel to pack it into the corners and firmly tap the outside of your wooden forms with a rubber mallet. The vibrations will settle the mix perfectly around your mold without collapsing the Dawn bubbles.
