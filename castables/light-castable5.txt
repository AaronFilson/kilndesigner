The plan has problems I’d fix whatever you decide, and the fix you asked about works. You can add a calcium aluminate cement set (the hydraulic, high-temperature type, not Portland) and stay under +10%, mostly because two ingredients in the current recipe aren’t earning their cost.

What’s wrong with the plan as written
The kyanite won’t do much. It only starts turning into mullite (the step that makes it expand) around 2010°F, and it needs roughly 2370–2640°F to finish. The plan’s 2100°F, one-hour firing barely starts it. In the 9" backup, most of the wall never gets that hot, so the 10% kyanite there just costs about $5 a batch. The “zero shrink” claim doesn’t hold up.
The backup will have a weak middle. Dextrin burns out around 600–900°F, and Greenstripe doesn’t sinter (fuse hard) until about 1800°F or more. Most of a 9" wall sits between those temperatures for its whole life. That zone ends up as dried clay with no binder, which crumbles. This is the strongest reason to add a set.
10% dextrin is about 5× a normal binder dose. It stays water-soluble and absorbs moisture, it tends to move toward the drying surface (hard skin, soft core), and the outer backup never gets hot enough to burn it off.
The 2" hot face has to stand alone while it’s unfired. A 48"-tall wall of foamed raw clay held up only by dextrin, fired with nothing behind it, has a real chance of slumping or collapsing.
The backup’s inner face runs hot. Using the thread’s own numbers, the joint between the layers sits around 2150°F. That’s fine chemically, since it’s the same fireclay, but the hot side of a foamed clay body will keep shrinking with repeated firings.
Smaller errors:
The thread calls CE70 a “mullite grog,” but earlier in the same thread it called CE70 a stoneware grog and said not to use it.
It says insulating brick is “fired under extreme pressure.” It isn’t; it’s made by burnout or foaming.
It prices CE70 at both $32 and $51 a bag.
The R-values and densities are estimates, not measurements.
Why calcium aluminate cement
Portland cement: out, as you said.
Sodium silicate: cheap, but the sodium acts as a strong flux at 2400°F and adds to the sodium from salt firing later.
Phosphoric acid / phosphate bond: strong at high temperature, but at 3–5% it busts the 10% budget, it’s unpleasant to handle, and it needs heat to set.
Colloidal silica: too expensive.
Calcium aluminate cement: this is what MIZZOU itself uses. Ordinary fireclay castables carry 15–25% of it and are still rated 2500–2600°F, so 6–10% in your mix is well inside normal practice.

What changes in the process
Water: with dextrin cut from 10% to 2%, the mix gets much thinner, so less water reaches the same thick “peanut butter” consistency. Start around 10 lb per 50 lb batch (20%) and adjust. Less water also means a stronger cement set.
Dextrin: sugars slow down this cement, and home-roasted dextrin contains some. Make a cup-sized test first. If it isn’t hard in 24 hours, drop the dextrin to zero. A pinch of lithium carbonate (about 0.03%, a potter’s material) speeds the set if you need it.
Curing: keep it damp under plastic for 24 hours at roughly 60–80°F. Don’t use heat lamps until after that; the cement needs the water.
Simpler build: the hot face sets in about a day and won’t dissolve. You can pour it, cure it, wet its back, and pour the backup in lifts a day apart. You no longer need the stand-alone pre-fire or the waterproofing step.
First firing: use a normal castable dry-out. Hold a long time near 220°F, then go about 50°F/hr through 400–1100°F.
Before committing to 60-plus batches, cast three test bricks (about 2×4×8") of each mix. Check that they set in 24 hours, weigh them for density, fire one to 2100°F and one to 2400°F, and measure the shrinkage and strength. These are reasonable starting recipes, but nobody has measured them.
