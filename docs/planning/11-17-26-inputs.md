## 2. The fire mouth while side stoking

**Needed for.** Fujiwara (the 5 in takiguchi alone can't finish a chamber; the book's 12 h matches
the mouth open), Bidai's and Tamba's mouth-shut corners (C1 3, C3b 1).

**In the repo.** Nothing beyond the book's figures.

**Outside.** General accounts say each chamber is stoked through its side holes once the one below
is done; none says whether the front mouth is shut, banked or left open. Musubi Kiln: "Kiln tenders
typically add fuel to additional fireboxes located at the bottom of each successive firing chamber"
(https://musubikiln.com/blogs/journal/the-enigmatic-science-of-japanese-climbing-kilns). MLIT's
Noborigama note says the same (https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001566318.pdf). Not found:
the practice for the front mouth.

**For the owner.** While side stoking, is the fire mouth (a) open, (b) banked, partly open, or
(c) bricked shut? v2 can carry it as a band from shut to open; the book's 12 h for Fujiwara sits at
"open".

**Answered (the owner, 2026-11-17).** It varies by kiln, so the fire mouth is modelled in all
three states: **open, partly open and closed**.
- Some kilns are fired with the mouth open. Their bottom chamber cools enough to unload while the
  chambers above are still firing.
- Others are covered for slow cooling.

What this means for v2:
1. The mouth's state is a setting in the design, per stage of the firing (stoking, side stoking,
   cooling). It isn't a constant.
2. Partly open is an open fraction of the mouth's area, from 0 (closed) to 1 (open).
3. Each kiln's state comes from the book where it says: Tamba open (pp.86–87) and Kitade closed
   (p.202), below. Where the book is silent, report the result for each state; don't pick one.
   This follows the rule that firing choices are options, not warnings. (Corrected 2026-11-18:
   this first gave Fujiwara's 12 h with the mouth open as the book's word. That match is v2's
   result, not the book's statement, and taking "open" for Fujiwara from it would fit the model to
   the kiln. Fujiwara is reported in every state.)
4. An open mouth lets cold air into the first chamber, so that chamber cools while the next ones
   fire. The cooling has to be modelled for the open case, so the "unload the bottom while the top
   fires" behaviour shows in the numbers.

**What the book says, by kiln (read on the page images, 2026-11-17;
`docs/research/olsen-figure-scaling.md` §2):**
- **Tamba is open.** "Eventually, [the] firebox becomes the air supply and stoking is done in the
  upper stoke [holes] and side stoke holes" (p.86). "During the firing of the last segment, the
  firebox and first segment [are] almost cool enough for unloading. The air for combustion is
  coming through the firebox, causing tremendously fast cooling in the first segment, and the
  shattering of quite a few pots. When the firing is [finished], the exit or chimney flues are
  plugged and the firebox sealed." (p.87). This is item 4's behaviour, printed.
- **Kitade is closed.** "Care must be taken, however, that the first chamber does not cool too
  quickly. Closing the front is very important." (p.202).
- **Olsen's own practice** closes the front chamber down and feeds the next chamber through an air
  tube under its grate (p.206).

What this changes:
1. **A stoke hole isn't an air intake.** v2 now takes each row's stage air through its side holes

2. **The hole's size matters only while it is open.** The fuel has to pass through it (a bundle
   needs 16 in, above), and cold air rushes in during each stoking. That inrush can be a short
   pulse per stoking, or left out and said so. It no longer sets the stage's air.
3. **Data to correct.** The two-chamber kiln's `secondary_air` of 49 in² is derived from the

   side door) is a hole size, not an intake.
4. **This mostly closes the side-hole question.** Where a row has no separate inlet, its stage
   air is the upstream gas alone. The open question becomes which rows have separate inlets,

## 4. Singh's low-fire kiln: the cover and the top temperature (p.155)

**Needed for.** Its open item (11–12 h; v2 levels near 807 °C with the top open to the room).

**In the repo.** The top is shards and saggar pieces in layers (pp.155–156). The 1760 °F (960 °C)
top is Falcone's, assumed. A 2–4 in clay lid would give 960 °C in 5.4–5.8 h.

**Outside.** Narrowed, not closed:
- Blue pottery (Jaipur and Delhi), a quartz-frit ware, is fired at 800–850 °C (secondary craft
  pages; the Handicrafts portal itself refused the connection).
- A 2019 Asian Age feature quotes a Delhi Blue Pottery trustee saying Jaipur's ware is heated to
  "1000 degrees" (https://www.asianage.com/life/more-features/081119/delhi-sculpts-the-blues.html;
  the excerpt is cut, not read in full).
- Neither names Singh's kiln, and no source gives the cover's thickness.

**For the owner.** (a) Top: take 800–1000 °C as the band (the frit ware's sources), in place of
Falcone's 960 °C? (b) Cover: carry it as a band from open (no cover) to a 2–4 in loose shard
layer? Any reading of its thickness closes it.

**Answered, the top (the owner, 2026-11-17).** Singh's top temperature is unknown. Model it as a
band **below 2100 °F (1149 °C)**.
- The upper end is the owner's 2100 °F.
- The lower end isn't given. The frit ware's 800 °C is the lowest the outside sources give, so
  the band runs 800 °C (1472 °F) to 1149 °C (2100 °F). This replaces Falcone's assumed 1760 °F.
- The test then asks: is there a top in that band that the kiln reaches in 11–12 h at some corner
  of its band? The open item is checked again after that.
- The cover: see below.

**Answered, the cover (the owner, 2026-11-17).** The cover is shards of pottery. It is different
each firing, but always porous: it holds heat and lets the smoke and flame through.

What this means for v2:
1. The cover isn't a lid or a wall. It is a **porous layer the flue gas leaves through**. The open
   top stays the kiln's exit, and the gas passes up through the shards.
2. It works in three ways:
   - it shields the chamber from radiating to the sky;
   - it stores heat;
   - the hot gas passing through heats it.
   v2 has no porous layer yet: today it has an open top or a solid wall.
3. Its thickness and open fraction change each firing, so they are a band. They run from a thin
   single layer to the 2–4 in used before, with the open fraction left as a stated band. No source
   gives either.
4. To source before building it: a packed-bed or porous-layer method for heat transfer from gas
   to solid and for radiation through the bed. The owner's description is the input, not the
   numbers.

**The cover's size (the owner, 2026-11-18).** "I estimate the shard layer to be 3" thick and
covering 80% of the width, so 20% is the chimney / exhaust port." So the cover is a lid of shards
3 in thick over 80 % of the top, with 20 % left open as the kiln's exhaust. Most of the gas leaves
through the 20 %. That makes it simpler than the packed bed taken before: a partial lid of
porous, fired shards plus an open port. Still to source: a loose shard layer's conductivity and
its emissivity.

**Applied (2026-11-18), in the data:**
- The top band: `top_temperature` [1472, 2100] °F in the dataset. The design takes 2100 °F, the
  band's top. The tests carry the band (`single_cases.TOP`).
- The cover estimate is a dataset fact and is in the design's header.
- The engine's open top doesn't take a cover yet, so T1 stays an open item (its reason restated).
  The plan lists the cover as engine work.

## 12. The lower-bound rule (owner decision)

C16's loads (IFB 10 psi, dense 25 psi) are carried hot, so they are lower bounds on hot strength.
A hot verdict that fails only on them is "not given", not "falls". Tobe's is the only one. Built,
waiting on a yes.

**Answered: yes (the owner, 2026-11-17).** Use the lower-bound rule. The bricks are at least that
good, or they couldn't be rated, but the makers don't list an upper bound on compressive strength.
So a load the rating proves is a floor on the strength, never its value. The rule is already in
the code (`v2/statics/sources.py` `ON_STAND_IN`; `test_statics_reference.py` then pinned Tobe hot
as "not given"), so nothing changes there. HWI's handbook (§11) now sources the 25 psi at
2460–2640 °F. Tobe hot was to stay "not given" until a hot strength was found or the
heat-to-statics bridge showed its joints cooler than the hot face; the owner's later answer,
below, replaced that for Tobe.

**Superseded for Tobe (the owner, 2026-11-17, later): it stood, so work backwards.** In the owner's
words: "This is the point of the exercise where I said that the reference kiln is truth. Whatever
it was made of, it took the temp and the pressure, so work backwards to find the properties." And:
"YOU DO NOT NEED TO FIND A PRINTED HOT CRUSH STRENGTH FOR A HISTORIC KILN DESTROYED IN 1963."
- **Brick:** high duty. Tobe was made in Japan in the 1800s or before, perhaps of tatami-rolled
  fireclay brick; nothing specific is known of the material.
- **Thickness:** not 9 in. "The brick could have been much thicker given the size and that it was
  pre-industrial." The design now takes 13½ in walls and ring, the next standard thickness.
- **The temperature falls through the brick.** "The temp does not go around all sides of the
  brick, that is just bad modeling." The hot statics' cap at every joint, as if the whole ring were
  at C16's temperature, is a model fault: the bridge from the heat model's wall temperatures is
  the fix (`02-plan.md`, next item 2).
- **The rule** goes into ADR 0017, decision 17, as amended: a reference kiln's own unknown
  property is worked back from it having stood, recorded as `inferred`, not withheld.

**Worked back (2026-11-17, v2's row statics, high-duty brick, seven chambers hot, μ 0.50):**

| Ring | Least hot crushing stress for an admissible state |
|---|---|
| 9 in | 30.5 psi (none at 30.50, one at 30.53): had the ring been 9 in, its brick carried this hot |
| about 9¾ in (9.74 no state, 9.77 stands) | 25 psi, C16's load |
| 13½ in (the design) | 19.5 psi (none at 19.50, one at 19.53) |
| 18 in | 17.0–17.3 psi |

The walls barely move it: at a 9 in ring, 30.5 psi with 9½ or 13½ in walls. So Tobe stands hot as
the design now has it, on C16's own load. The dataset records the two strengths as `inferred`
facts (`reference_kilns.yaml`, tobe). The tests pin them to ±0.1 psi:
`test_statics_row.py::test_tobe_is_worked_back_from_standing` and the reference set, where Tobe
now stands. The "not given" rule stays for designs no one has built.

## 15. Concrete's specific heat (EN 1992-1-2 §3.3.2): closed

Read from the third-party copy of BS EN 1992-1-2:2004 already cited as [K31] in `blocked-items.md`
(https://www.phd.eng.br/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/en.1992.1.2.2004.pdf), pp.26–27, text layer
extracted with pypdf:

> (1) The specific heat cp(θ) of dry concrete (u = 0%) may be determined from the following:
> Siliceous and calcareous aggregates:
> cp(θ) = 900 (J/kg K) for 20 °C ≤ θ ≤ 100 °C
> cp(θ) = 900 + (θ − 100) (J/kg K) for 100 °C < θ ≤ 200 °C
> cp(θ) = 1000 + (θ − 200)/2 (J/kg K) for 200 °C < θ ≤ 400 °C
> cp(θ) = 1100 (J/kg K) for 400 °C < θ ≤ 1200 °C

> (2) Where the moisture content is not considered explicitly in the calculation method, the
> function given for the specific heat of concrete with siliceous or calcareous aggregates may be
> modelled by a constant value, cp.peak, situated between 100 °C and 115 °C with linear decrease
> between 115 °C and 200 °C.
> cp.peak = 900 J/kg K for moisture content of 0 % of concrete weight
> cp.peak = 1470 J/kg K for moisture content of 1,5 % of concrete weight
> cp.peak = 2020 J/kg K for moisture content of 3,0 % of concrete weight
> And linear relationship between (115 °C, cp.peak) and (200 °C, 1000 J/kg K).
