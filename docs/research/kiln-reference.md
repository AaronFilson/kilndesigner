## 1. Arches and skewbacks

- **Rise.** Minimum about 1½ in per ft of span, what a featheredge skewback gives; less "can
  result in structural difficulty". Maximum "generally" 3 in/ft. More rise is sturdier; he has
  built 6 in/ft, so "no hard-and-fast rule" (p.51, Read). Domes and crowns 2½–3 in/ft (p.51).
  Wide spans: at least 1¾ in/ft (p.296, Read). For chamber kilns of 4–8 ft span he uses
  4–6 in/ft (p.296, Read). p.296 calls 1½ in/ft "the minimum" and says to use "as great a rise as
  possible starting from the lower limit" (p.296, Read).
- **Rise as a ratio**: 1½ in/ft "(1 in 8)"; 3 in/ft "(1 in 4)"; 4–6 in/ft "(1 in 3 to 1 in 2)"
  (p.296, Read).
- **Catenary.** Its rise-to-span ratio is usually above the 1½–3 in/ft range that suits sprung
  arches. Lay it out by hanging a cord or chain from the two span points. It is self-supporting:
  wall and arch in one (p.50, Read).
- **Standard rises and their skewbacks** (p.53, p.296–297, Read):
  - 1½ in/ft: a 9 in featheredge (2½ in series).
  - 1.608 in/ft: a 60° arch, inside radius equal to the span; special 60° skewbacks come 4½, 9 and
    13½ in thick.
  - 2.302 in/ft: standard 9 in side and end skews, the "48°" skews of the 2½ and 3 in series
    (p.296).
  - Featheredge arch form radius = 1.0625 × span (p.50); Fig. A-2 prints 1.0628 (p.296). Table
    A-3.1's radii use 1.0625: a 1 ft span gives 1 ft 0¾ in (derived) (p.298).
  - p.297 names "the four established rises": 1½, 1.608, 2 (?) and 2.302 in/ft. Using the
    standard arches of Tables A-2 to A-7 is "wiser and easier" than cutting bricks to the formulas
    (p.297, Read).
- **Skewback assemblies** (p.25, Read):
  - a 4½ in arch at 1½ in/ft: one 9 × 4½ × 2 in brick plus one 9 in featheredge (2½ in series);
  - a 9 in arch at 2 5/16 in/ft: two 9 in end skews and two 9 in side skews (2½ in series) plus
    one 9 × 2¼ × 2¼ in soap;
  - Figs 1-11 and 1-12 label the featheredge 9, 4½ and 2½ in, with "5"" (?) on its sloping face,
    and the 9 in assembly 9 in at the base, 7½ in high and 4½ in;
  - "The most commonly used skewback assemblies for building kilns (from 10 to 100 cubic feet in
    size) are assemblies that give a rise to the arch of 1 1/2 inches per foot of span" (read by
    the user from the book, 2026-10-29; lost in the binding in the scan).
- A 1½ in/ft skewback can be cut from a standard 9 in straight (2½ in series): saw it diagonally
  across the 4½ × 2½ in face, down the 9 in length (p.53, Read).
- **Special 60° skewbacks**, Fig. A-3 (p.297, Read; read off the drawings):
  - 4½ in arch, rise 1 19/32 in/ft: 9 in long, 4½ in (?) at the back, top 2¼ in, toe 19/32 in;
  - 4½ in arch, rise 2 in/ft: 9 in long, 4½ in at the back, top 1 13/16 in, toe 29/32 in;
  - 9 in arch, rise printed "2" per foot of rise": 4½ in wide, 8 in (?) at the back, top
    3 19/32 in, base 9 in, toe 13/16 in;
  - 9 in arch, rise 1½ in/ft: 4½ in wide, 9 in at the back, top 4¾ in, base 9 in, toe 1 1/16 in;
  - 9 in arch, rise 1 19/32 in/ft: 4½ in wide, 9 in at the back, top 4½ in, base 9 in, toe
    1 7/32 in.
  - The figure labels the 60° rise 1 19/32 in/ft, which is 1.594 (derived); the text says 1.608.
- **Arch geometry** (p.296, Read): r = (S² + 4H²)/(8H); S = 2r sin(θ/2); arc lengths 2πrθ/360.
  Also H = r − ½√(4r² − S²) and R = r + T, with R the outside radius and T the thickness;
  π = 3.1416. A second form of S is printed "2√H[2(r − R)]", which is garbled (Flagged).
- **Table A-2, rises per foot of span** (p.298, Read). Multiply the radius factor by the span to
  get the inside radius.

- **Brick counts by formula** (p.296, Read): bricks in the outer arc = L ÷ "2½ or 3½in." by series
  (3½ is probably a misprint for 3: Flagged); total taper = L − l (printed "L − 1"); tapered
  bricks = total taper ÷ the taper of one brick; spacing of the tapered bricks among straights =
  bricks in L ÷ tapered bricks. The printed brick-count tables are in §20.
- **Thickness.** 4½, 6¾ or 9 in and up, by brick and span; **no rule ties thickness to span**
  (p.296, Read). Suspended arches are usually 4½ or 9 in (p.48). A cast or rammed arch on a kiln
  over 9 ft³ should be at least 4½ in thick (p.40); small rammed arches and catenaries are rammed
  4 in (10 cm) thick or more (p.40, Read). Cast domes: "a 6in. (15cm) thickness is best" for most
  castings, and 4½ in (11.5 cm) "is adequate" for small ones (p.59, Checked on an enhanced crop);
  the 6 in is for domes "10 to 12 ft (3 to 3.6m)" across (read by the user, 2026-10-29). 5–6 in on
  his wood kilns
  (p.186–188; p.187, Read). One cast dome was first cast too thin and had to be thickened with
  more castable over the top (p.124, Read).
- **Bricks for each thickness** (p.46–47, p.296, Read):
  - 4½ in: arch bricks, or arch bricks with straights;
  - 6¾ in: large 9 in arch bricks, or those with large 9 in straights;
  - 9 in: wedges, or wedges with straights. Keys (or keys with straights) are uncommon and not
    recommended, because the load bears on the narrow face;
  - over 9 in: special wedges; 13½ in wedges are standard from some makers.
  - No.1, No.2 and No.3 arch bricks each turn a circle on their own. Mixed with each other and
    with straights they turn almost any circle (p.46, Read).
- **Kinds of sprung arch** (p.47, Read):
  - bonded: the most common and the best; staggered joints, so neighbours carry the load if a few
    bricks fail;
  - ring: one failed brick drops the whole ring and is hard to replace; easy to lay with standard
    shapes;
  - ribbed: the ribs keep it stable as the bricks between them erode;
  - straight.
- **Arch of straights** (p.48, Read): each brick's inside edge touches the outside edge of the one
  below. Lay both sides together to the top and cut the key to fit, with the mortar wedge-shaped.
  Spring it off the form by driving small refractory wedges into the joint gaps from the bottom
  up; over-wedging opens the bottom joints and can drop a brick. A crown can be wedged with broken
  brick, every other course started with a half-brick for the bond (p.145, Read).
- **Suspended arch** (p.48–49, Read): it hangs from a metal frame on rods through holes or grooves,
  pipe or tee bar (Fig. 2-22 shows a threaded, bolted stainless rod). It isn't tied into the side
  walls, so it takes expansion both ways and is easy to repair, unless the side walls need
  bracing. It costs more than a sprung arch. Used on top-loading electric or gas kilns.
- **Arch forms** (p.49, Read): one-use, Masonite in 12 in strips nailed to slats, on 2 × 2 or
  2 × 4 in supports with plywood plates; reusable, ¾–1 in plywood ribs with 1 × 2 to 1 × 3 in
  slats 1 in apart, and a centre rib if needed. Arch boards are joined by slat runners; today
  masonite, plywood or milled 1 × 3 in timber (p.113, Read).
- **Openings** (p.49, Read): use angle-mitred spanning bricks (cut skews plus a key) for larger
  flue holes and damper slots. A corbel arch spans up to 24 in (61 cm), for portholes and flue
  holes too wide for a lintel.
- **Corbel roofs are poor practice** (p.49, Read): he has seen 10–16 ft³ kilns roofed this way.
  They are structurally unstable, their uneven inside surface heats unevenly and stirs flame
  turbulence, and they erode faster.
- **Domes and crowns** (p.51, Read): a dome is part of one sphere, usually plugged at the top; a
  crown is two sphere radii from two axes meeting at dead top centre, usually with a flue at the
  top. Either can be laid in header or stretcher courses. A swinging-arm dome gauge (credited to
  "Hendo", Fig. 2-28A) sets each course's pitch. IFB can be cut to any radius with a wood saw, and
  a tight fit strengthens the arch; dense brick needs standard shapes or a brick saw. He would
  rather cast a dome or crown in IFB castable (p.51, p.58; §4).
- **Thrust.** When skewback, wall and arch make an acute angle, steel must hold the thrust (p.53,
  Fig. 2-35A). When the angle is greater, the stress runs down a slanted (battered) wall and the
  arch is self-supporting (p.53, Fig. 2-35B, Read). The skewback must sit level on the wall, or the
  arch's angle is compromised. The first ("throw") brick's lip must rest on the wall, with a cut
  wedge or jamb under it if needed: a springline flush with the wall face slips (p.53, Figs 2-36,
  2-37). A common wall between two chambers carries two arches' thrust: build it 4½ in thicker
  than the others, never narrower than its two skewbacks (p.46). Bracing the firebox and front
  chamber "is a must" on chamber kilns (p.97).
- **Thrust in practice** (Read):
  - early domed, downdraft and beehive kilns had curved walls 18–22 in (46–56 cm) thick, some 3 or
    even 5 ft, which carried the dome's thrust with no reinforcing (p.45);
  - buttress masonry goes behind a castable skewback (p.58, Fig. 2-50);
  - buttress the side walls "extremely well" before striking a dome form (p.59), or with dirt
    reinforced with rocks and concrete (p.186);
  - on a climbing kiln, brace the first chamber's front wall while arching, and set the arch peak
    back from the chamber centre-line so its thrust goes to the common wall and on to the chimney
    (p.95).
  - **No page in the scan gives a thrust formula or table.**
- A ring arch on one of his chamber kilns opened almost ½ in during firing; a steel tie might
  have helped (p.47, Read).
- A heavy door on undersized angle iron can torque the arch and break its bond (p.175, Read).

## 2. Expansion, frames and steel

- **Expansion.** Each foot of refractory wall grows 1/16–3/32 in (1–2 mm) heating and cooling
  (p.51, Checked). That is 0.52–0.78 %, which agrees with IFB's 0.5–0.7 % reversible expansion
  (thermal-properties.md §2). On p.52 the Fig. 2-31 caption gives 1/16 in as "(1mm)" and the text
  as "(1.5mm)"; 1/16 in is 1.6 mm (derived). The inch values agree (p.52, Read).
- **Allowance in a steel frame** (p.52–53, Read):
  - leave 1/16 in per foot of wall: a 5 ft wall gets 5/32 in at each end; offset the joint, don't
    run it straight through; Fig. 2-31 fills it with a ¼ in fiber pad mortared to the brick end;
  - in hard-brick walls only the 9 in hot-face course needs the floating corner joint, filled with
    expansion-joint board or ceramic fiber (p.52, Fig. 2-30);
  - an IFB kiln in a flexible frame (sheet metal or expanded steel backing), built not too tight
    and not wedged into the frame, needs no joints for walls up to 5 ft (150 cm) by the text
    (p.53), but only up to 48 in by Fig. 2-32's label (p.52). The caption misprints the limit as
    "4 in. (10.2 cm)" (Flagged). Use 4 ft, the cautious value;
  - walls over 5 ft "can, and in most cases should" get ¼ in fiber pads at the corners (p.53);
    well over 5 ft: vertical cuts filled with fiber;
  - Fig. 2-32's IFB corner: K-26 stretchers on the hot face, a K-20 rowlock back-up course, a
    sheet-metal side wall and an angle-iron corner post (p.52);
  - free-standing chamber kilns use no expansion joints: the walls act as one structural unit, and
    only the steel frame's tolerance matters (p.52);
  - an unframed kiln's tie rods are bolted taut at room temperature (p.52); a frame with no
    tolerance bends and the walls crack.
- **No joints at all.** He casts irregular domes without expansion joints and relies on the
  material to take the stress, though it "may not be what ceramic engineers recommend" (p.60,
  Read). The Bizen kiln has none either; it copes by a long, slow firing and cooling (p.100,
  Read).
- **Frame sizes**, updraft kiln 5 × 5 × 6 ft outside (p.174, Checked): corners 3×3×¼ in angle; top
  and bottom ties 2×2×¼; floor perimeter 4×4×¼; floor joists 2×2×¼; door frame 3×3×¼; skewback
  brace 2×2×¼. They "can be larger (stronger), but not smaller" for this size; smaller kilns can
  use 3/16 in angle; larger kilns may need thicker, especially the floor and door. Fig. 6-69, which
  draws these members, is captioned "Angle-iron bracing for 4 x 4ft (122 x 122cm) kiln"; the
  text's 5 × 5 × 6 ft governs (p.174, Flagged).
- His fast-fire kiln's frame is 2×2×3/16 in angle, bolted, with flexboard inside it (p.139,
  Fig. 5-36, Checked).
- **Building the frame** (Read): weld the angle-iron frame to the brick dimensions (p.137). Mitre
  joints suit one or two kilns; butt joints suit quantity, being "far stronger" and more accurate,
  and can be cut in a metal press cutter (p.248–249). A small oil downdraft was built on a welded
  frame to make it portable (p.138).
- **Lighter kilns, lighter steel** (Read): IFB weighs 65–85 % less than dense firebrick, which
  reduces the need for heavy foundations and structural steel (p.25); a flat fiber roof lightens
  the kiln and allows lighter steel (p.150).
- **Heat and the frame** (p.175–176, Read): keep 12 in between the hot face and door-frame steel
  (2½ in burned and warped one commercial frame); floor joists fatigue and sag at 600–800 °F. A
  ring pilot or ignition match under the kiln may stay lit only if it is designed and
  heat-shielded so it doesn't overheat the space under the kiln (p.175). Never leave the ignitor
  trailer ring on under an updraft, not even to preheat: floor joists deflect and lose strength
  above 400 °F (204 °C), and a loaded floor can drop (p.239, Read). Weld with long beads, not
  spots. A 4×4 ft door needs at least 3 hinges, each 9 in or longer. Never cinch a door tight:
  expansion breaks the jamb.
- **Doors** (Read):
  - jamb seal (p.175, Figs 6-70, 6-71): notch the door 1–2 in into the jamb (labels 4½–6 in; a
    second version has a 2 in step with 8–9 in). The bad design is a frame enclosing the bricks
    with 0 in overlap (4.5–7 in);
  - lock (p.176, Figs 6-74, 6-75): a steel pipe welded to the corner of the jamb frame holds a
    steel round (about ¾–1 in (?)) whose elliptical end bears on the door when turned. Keep the
    elliptical end within 1 in (2.5 cm) of the jamb corner, or the rod bends and the door loosens;
    too long a rod bends and breaks the seal;
  - car kiln (p.172): hang the door from an overhead I-beam on two half-ton trolleys with U-bolts
    and eye-bolts; spring-tension latches pull it into the jambs;
  - castable doors: §4; fiber seals: §5.
- **Flat roofs** (Read): a flat roof needs rods through drilled bricks bolted to the frame, or
  cinched corner bolts, and "constant surveillance" as they loosen (p.177). On electric kilns an
  angle-iron frame is "a basic requirement": rows of ring bricks held by tie bolts through the
  bricks (Fig. 9-17), or a roof cast in an angle-iron frame with crossbar reinforcing rods
  (Fig. 9-19) (p.248–249). A small portable wood kiln has a flat roof of rowlock bricks held by an
  iron frame (p.147). Flat roof against arch: §11.

## 3. Walls and materials

### Dense refractories

- **High-silica brick** (up to 90 % silica) reacts with the basic soda flux in a salt kiln and
  slags; don't use it there. It is also less stable through heating and cooling than other
  materials (p.15, Read).
- **Oxide-bonded SiC** resists oxidation poorly or not at all below 2192 °F (1200 °C): it forms
  SiO₂, grows unevenly, cristobalite breaks the silica layer, and over time it cracks and warps.
  So it is unwise in low-fire oxidation; it does much better at higher temperatures (p.15–16,
  Read). Norton's Crystolon 63 oxynitride bond greatly increases SiC's resistance to soda slag
  (p.15 footnote, Read).
- **Melting points**, Table 1-1 (p.16, Read). The °C/°F figures are printed one row below their
  material; read with that offset, kaolinite comes out at 1785 °C / 3245 °F, as the text says.
  SiC 2300 °C (4170 °F); Cr₂O₃ 2275 (4127); spinel 2135 (3875); Al₂O₃ 1900–2000 (3450–3630);
  forsterite 1910 (3470); fused mullite 1840 (3340); kaolinite 1785 (3245); cristobalite 1728
  (3142). Higher up: HfC 3890 °C, TaC 3870, ZrC 3540, carbon 3500, tungsten 3410, ThO₂ 3300, TiC
  3140, ZrB₂ 3060, titanium nitride (printed "Ti(NO₃)4") 2950, TiB₂ 2900, UO₂ 2760, fused ZrO₂
  2700, fused MgO 2620, lime 2570, B₄C 2400. Alumina at 1900–2000 °C looks low (Flagged).
- **Mullite refractories** must have alumina between 56 and 79 %, at most 5 % other metallic
  impurities and at most 5 % deformation (p.17, Read). Mullite comes from calcined kyanite; pure
  kaolin melts at 3245 °F (1785 °C) (p.16, Read).
- **Dense brick**, ASTM classes by PCE (p.17–19, Read), with the PCE as temperatures (p.17):
  - super-duty 33–34+: 3169–3205 °F (1743–1763 °C);
  - high-duty 32–32+: 3123–3135 °F (1717–1724 °C);
  - intermediate 28–31: 2995–3061 °F (1646–1683 °C);
  - low-duty 28 and down: 2995 °F (1646 °C).
  - Low and intermediate suit potters' kilns except fireboxes. Intermediate-duty is well suited to
    about cone 10; a low-duty brick of PCE 28 stands cone 10 in a kiln but not in a firebox or
    another extreme hot face (p.18, Read). Yet Fig. 5-39 builds the fast-fire kiln's own fireboxes
    of "LOW DUTY FIREBRICK", 90 bricks (Flagged: the book disagrees with itself; the examples
    follow p.18 for floors that roof a firebox).
  - The book disagrees with itself: Table 1-2 (p.19) prints minimum PCEs of 31½ for high-duty, 29
    for intermediate and 15 for low-duty; Table 1-9A (p.34, plastics) gives high-duty 31 and
    super-duty 32 (Flagged). If the engine checks PCE, take it from ASTM C27, not from these pages.

### Insulating firebrick (IFB)

- **IFB grades**, ASTM C155-70 (Table 1-3, p.26, Read). The table has no rows for grades 24 or 25.

  | Group | Max temperature | Bulk density, lb/ft³ | kg/m³ | Reheat change ≤ 2 % when tested at |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 16 | 1620 °F | 34 | 545 | 1550 °F (845 °C) |
  | 20 | 2000 °F | 40 | 641 | 1950 °F (1065 °C) |
  | 23 | 2300 °F | 48 | 769 | 2250 °F (1230 °C) |
  | 26 | 2600 °F | 54 | 865 | 2550 °F (1400 °C) |
  | 28 | 2800 °F | 60 | 961 | 2750 °F (1520 °C) |
  | 30 | 3000 °F | 68 | 1089 | 2950 °F (1620 °C) |
  | 32 | 3200 °F | 95 | 1522 | 3150 °F (1730 °C) |
  | 33 | 3300 °F | 95 | 1522 | 3250 °F (1790 °C) |

- IFB conducts ⅙–⅓ as much as dense brick (p.25). It is made by burnout of an organic filler
  (except A.P. Green's Greenlite, a lightweight aggregate), by casting, stiff-mud or dry-press,
  then fired and machined to size (p.25, Read).
- **What each grade is for** (p.25–27, Read):
  - 16: to 1620 °F (882 °C), printed "with a 2000 °F (1093 °C) back-up"; strictly a back-up brick,
    though usable in a raku kiln;
  - 20: mainly a back-up brick; good cold-crushing and hot-load strength; the same composition and
    melting point as 23, 2750 °F (1510 °C), except 0.6 % more CaO and 0.1 % more alkali (Na₂O);
  - 23: excellent for kilns to cone 6 and for cold spots in kilns to cone 10; unofficially usable
    in cone 10 kilns, never above cone 10;
  - 24: "24 HS" (heat storage), the same melting point as 23 and 20; Olsen has never used it;
  - 25 (2500 °F/1371 °C): fills the gap between 23 and 26 in temperature and price; JM-25 is
    coarser, easier to cut and mortar, and very strong; "a good choice for high-fire ceramic kilns
    for the potter";
  - 26 (2600 °F/1427 °C): excellent for cone 10; coarser, easier to cut, shape and mortar (it
    absorbs mortar less than 23 or 25). K-26 on the hot face of his anagama, over 25 years,
    resisted wood-ash slag "superbly", better than hard brick of the same rating (p.26);
  - 28 (2800 °F/1538 °C): excellent for fireboxes and kiln floors, for its high spalling
    resistance;
  - 30 (3000 °F/1649 °C): extremely high-temperature kilns and furnaces, cone 15 porcelain; 32 and
    33 are also for extreme temperatures;
  - most US companies now sell IFB made in China (p.27).

### Brick sizes and shapes

### Mortar

- Joints 1/32 in for IFB, at most 1/16 in for dense brick; dip mix 24 US qt water per 100 lb,
  butter mix 14 qt; 300–400 lb per 1000 bricks (p.31–33, Read).
- **What mortar does** (p.30, Read): its chemistry must match the brick's. It bonds the brickwork
  into a monolith that resists mechanical and thermal shock, makes the kiln gas-tight, beds
  irregular bricks and stops slag entering the joints. Three groups: ready-mixed air-setting, dry
  air-setting, dry heat-setting.
- **ASTM classes** (p.31, Read): super-duty 2910 °F (1600 °C); high-duty 2730 °F (1500 °C);
  medium-duty 2550 °F (1400 °C). ASTM C178-47: free of grit; spreads with a trowel as supplied or
  after moderate tempering; thins to dipping consistency with water; stays workable six months
  after opening.
- **Air-setting** mortar reaches its full strength above 1500 °F (816 °C) and bonds through the
  whole joint, not just near the hot face. From the drum it is trowelled about 1/16 in (2 mm)
  thick and the bricks are tapped into place (p.31, Read).
- **Heat-setting** mortar sinters only where it reaches its setting temperature, giving joints of
  minimum shrinkage. Use it in arches, domes and walls that mix bricks of different thermal
  behaviour: the bricks can move and equalize stress in the first firing before it sets
  (p.31–32, Read).
- **Dipping IFB** (p.32, Fig. 1-13, Read): 2600/2800 IFB, thin the mortar so a brick dips a
  quarter of the way down, not so thick it oozes out; lighter 2000/2500 IFB, a third of the way,
  creamy, and thick enough not to dry before laying. Mix with warm water an hour before use, and
  always mix a full bag. His kits lay IFB 2500/2600 in Harbison-Walker Sairset air-set mortar.
- **Don't pre-soak 2000/2500 IFB** (p.32, Read): sodium silicate leaches out and crystallizes on
  the joints, which slag like salt glaze at the first high firing; very humid weather does the
  same. Scrape crystals off dry, never with a wet rag. Thermal Ceramics "smooth-set" had this
  problem (said to be fixed); "air-set" dries faster and leaches less.
- **Mortar IFB rather than laying it dry** (p.32, Read): dry-laid bricks spall, crack, grind
  smaller (especially 2600/2800) and move, and can fall out of an arch. Dry laying only saves work
  and allows tear-down, and many bricks are lost when such a kiln is taken down.
- Never mortar over dried mortar; scrape the old off first. Reseal an opened can under a little
  water (p.33, Read).
- **Homemade mortars** work but are weaker and less durable than prepared ones (p.33, Read):
  Olsen's, fire clay with 40 % fire-clay grog; Chappell's, 2 sand : 2 [grog?] : 1 stoneware clay;
  Hotchkiss's, 50 % Lincoln fire clay and 50 % sand.

### Walls, bonds and openings

- **Unsupported wall height** (p.41, Read): 4½ in to 3 ft; 9 in (headers and stretchers
  alternating) to 8 ft; 13½ in to 12 ft. All-stretcher walls not above 3 ft unless supported.
- **Header courses** expose the least hot face per brick, 4½ × 2½ in, and keep each brick's back
  relatively cool, so they suit hot-face bricks. A mainly-header wall normally has three or four
  header courses to one stretcher course, which is more rigid than all headers (p.41, Read).
- **Mainly-stretcher walls** normally have three stretcher courses to one header course (Fig. 2-4
  draws four). In wood or salt kilns this lets you replace a slagged 4½ × 9 in hot-face brick, or
  tie a 4½ in ram-mix skin wall into the remaining bricks (p.42, Read).
- **English bond**, alternate header and stretcher courses in 9 or 13½ in walls, is extremely
  stable and good practice. Joints must not line up vertically. Start a 9 in wall's pattern with a
  9 × 6 in large brick, a 4½ in bat and straights (p.42–43, Read). A rowlock course is bricks on
  edge, showing 2½ × 9 in; a soldier course is bricks on end, showing 4½ × 2½ in (p.43, Read).
- **13½ in walls**, three ways (p.44, Read): 9 × 4½ × 2½ in bricks with 9 × 6 × 2½ in bricks, no
  through joints; four courses of hot-face stretchers backed by headers, then one course of headers
  backed by stretchers, easier to repair; or a 13½ in brick in every fifth-course header course,
  joints alternating each course: very stable, easy to repair and "good for setting sprung
  arches".
- **Flue holes** go on a header course, with the header above the opening laid so it can be
  knocked out if the flue proves too small. A 9 in flue separator alternates header and stretcher
  courses. Err on flues too large rather than too small (p.45, Read). The flue holes for the 3 and
  2½ in series: "4 1/2 x 10in. (11.5 x 25cm)" (read by the user, 2026-10-29; the left margin is
  lost in the scan).
- **Curved walls** (domed, downdraft and beehive kilns) are stronger than straight ones: the curve
  wedges the bricks against falling in, and the height is limited only by the crushing strength of
  the bottom bricks (p.45, Read). Bricks by thickness: 4½ in, circle bricks (arch bricks possible
  but not recommended); 9 in, key bricks, or circle and key bricks; 13½ in and more, key bricks, or
  circle and key bricks (wedges not recommended). Alternate the joints as in straight walls.
  Fig. 2-13 (p.45, Checked on the page image 2026-10-30) draws a curved wall of rectangular bricks:
  in plan, a two-brick-thick wall whose bricks are square-ended, so the joints between them open
  wider toward the outside; in perspective, a stretcher course on a header course, both of straights
  following the curve. The caption: "Joints are alternated in the construction of a curved wall."
- **Walls in practice** (Read):
  - IFB kilns: a 7 in wall is sufficient (p.176): a hot-face stretcher course, presumably IFB (the
    word is cut off at the gutter), behind which is a rowlock course of low-duty brick or a 2 in (?)
    low-duty insulating fiberboard (p.176). Fig. 2-32 draws 4½ in K-26 with a 2½ in K-20 rowlock
    (p.52); Table 1-12 uses 4½ in K-26 with 2½ in K-23 (p.39; §5).
  - Climbing kilns 9–10 in, loosely built with vapor holes along the top of each chamber; Bizen
    10–13 in, tight, with no vapor holes and an earth overcoat, which cuts heat loss, soaks more
    evenly and saves fuel (p.99–100). A Kyoto-style climbing kiln was overcoated with 3 in
    (7.5 cm) of fireclay, dirt and straw (p.95).
  - Fiber: §5. Electric kilns: §18.

### Kiln furniture

- **Shelf weights**, 12 × 24 in (p.33–35, Read): his mullite shelves, 1 in thick, about 20.5 lb
  (9.3 kg); his conventional SiC, 1 in, about 22 lb (10 kg); cordierite up to 21 lb (9.5 kg);
  Advancer (nitride-bonded SiC) 8 lb (3.6 kg).
- **Conventional SiC** warps less and has a higher service temperature, but oxidizes below 1200 °C
  (2192 °F), and in oxidizing atmospheres at higher temperatures (p.33–34, Read).
- **Saint-Gobain/Norton SiC furniture** (p.34, Read): recrystallized R-SiC (Crystar 2000) is
  porous and slowly oxidizes, but above 2462 °F (1350 °C) its microstructure resists oxidation
  better; sintered S-SiC is the strongest and most oxidation-resistant, and very expensive;
  siliconized Si-SiC has its pores filled with silicon and must be **used below 2462 °F (1350 °C)
  only**, because the free silicon may melt above that.
- **Nitride-bonded SiC (Advancer)** (p.35, Read): fired in nitrogen; almost twice as strong as
  recrystallized SiC; low mass and high conductivity allow shorter cycles; thin ("5/16 in" (?));
  glaze-resistant. Excellent in gas kilns, usable in soda firing (low porosity); **in wood kilns,
  best in the back chambers**. It is very sensitive to moisture and may fail if fired wet. Thin
  nitride-bonded shelves near the inlet flue and in the direct flame of his fast-fire wood kiln
  cracked from the very fast rise and the burst of heat after each stoking (p.35).
- **Table 1-10**, Saint-Gobain, faint scan (p.35, Read): cordierite, maximum 2372 °F (1300 °C),
  cone 10; porosity 30.1 % (?); strength 1450 psi at 68 °F and 1305 psi (?) at "1250 °F" (?);
  conductivity 8 BTU·in/h·ft²·°F; alumina 46, silica 43.8, magnesia 6.2, other 4.0 %. Advancer,
  maximum 2642 °F (1450 °C), cone 16 (?); strength 24500 psi (?) and 25500 psi (?); conductivity
  125 (?); SiC 70 %, silicon-nitride bond 30 %.
- **Wadding** (p.117, Read): fireclay and alumina with coarse silica sand and sawdust or rice hulls,
  best for separating pots; or equal parts kaolin and alumina, best for shelves, posts and grate
  bars.

### Raw-clay construction

- **Raw bricks** (p.54, Read): make them in a single or multiple wood press mould, or a long trough
  with piano-hinged sides and sizing slots for a cutting wire; the trough should be at least as
  long as the kiln is wide. Pack in clay, drop the sides, dry the bricks and cut sizes to suit the
  kiln. The body used: 50 % Lincoln 60 fireclay and 50 % coarse sand; the mortar is a slip of the
  same body (p.54, p.33).
- **Laying** (p.54, Read): lay bone-dry raw bricks with a soft fireclay brick mix as mortar, the
  consistency of very soft clay (not trowelling or dipping consistency), to level and seal the
  joints.
- **Building a multi-chamber raw-clay kiln** (p.55–57, Read): build from the top chamber down;
  shape the dry blocks with an axe, including the key row; rope-strap each arch until the arch
  below is built to buttress it; dry each chamber with a small fire as it is finished. One such
  kiln had its top two chambers bisque-fired before the rest was finished, and was still firing
  after 30 years (p.33, p.56). Take care in the first firing so a fast rise doesn't blow out the
  raw blocks (p.57).

## 4. Castables and cast domes

- **Mixing and placing** (p.28, Read): mix in a mortar box or paddle mixer for at least six
  minutes (read by the user, 2026-10-29) with clean, cool water (cement mixers work). Use the exact water ratio;
  mix and place within about 30 minutes of adding water. Vibrate deep forms: dense castables at
  high frequency (an internal poker is best: less water, more strength, density and abrasion
  resistance); insulating castables at medium frequency and lightly, or rodded by hand until they
  settle with a wet look. Over-vibration drives binder and fines to the surface. Waterproof any
  porous surface the cast touches (oil, grease, plastic, shellac, Ceramul).
- **Curing.** These are two rules, which the earlier "cure at least 18–24 h damp" merged:
  castables set in 8 h or less, then need **at least 24 h of water curing** under damp cloth and
  then plastic (p.28, Read); a fiber-type castable needs **at least 18 h of air-curing**, then can
  be force-dried at up to 400 °F (204 °C) (p.40, Read). Cast domes: 24 h under damp towels and
  plastic (p.59, p.188).
- **Weather and handling** (p.29, Read): hotter weather sets faster, cooler slower. Never cast in
  freezing weather or let a cast freeze before it has cured. Castables can be poured, gunned (dry
  or wet gun) or slap-trowelled. Don't overwork or smooth the surface: it slumps, especially over
  arch and dome forms.
- **Ball-in-hand test** (p.29, p.58, Read): toss a ball of the mix a few inches. If it breaks up,
  it is too dry (add water); if it flattens ("pancakes"), too wet (add material); if it holds its
  shape, about right. A hand-slap test is also used (p.187).

### Cast domes

- **Skewback** (p.58, Read): wedge IFB to slope in for two or three rows, the last row slanted to
  fit the dome; 2500 or 2600 °F IFB is best for the slanted bricks and those below. Fig. 2-50:
  castable on a K-25/26 featheredge over K-25/26 straights wedged as the skew section ("arch or
  wedge can be used"), a vertical wall, buttress masonry behind, and a "THRUST" arrow. On a brick
  base, lay the 9 in walls straight up, then slant header courses inward as the transition to the
  castable (p.186, Read).
- **Form** (p.58, p.186–187, Read): a braced, collapsible platform up to the slanted bricks; the
  dome shaped in dirt and capped with wet sand; plastic over the form and the skewback brick. No
  porous surface may touch the castable: it "has to be cast on a non-porous surface" (p.187).
- **Placing** (p.58, p.187, Read): place each batch next to or on the last, and do the whole
  casting in one session. Slap the castable on from the skewback, work around, then up; keep the
  receiving edge slanted in and down, a ledge pointing down into the form for the next layer.
- **Workmanship** (p.59, p.187, Read): overworking draws water and fine cement to the surface,
  sealing it and leaving a weak skin that can loosen in firing; too much agitation slumps the
  castable and makes the dome uneven. Don't smooth it with a trowel or a hand; leave it coarse.
- Gauge the thickness with chopsticks poked through to the form (p.59, Read). Thicknesses: §1.
- **Curing and capping** (p.59, p.188, Read): cover with damp towels and plastic, cure 24 h,
  uncover. Then cap with a mix of dirt, sawdust and cement (like adobe), or dig the form dirt out
  of the chamber and heap it over the kiln as insulation.
- **Before striking the form**, buttress the side walls "extremely well" with masonry: once the
  platform is out, the walls carry the casting's weight (p.59, Read). Dirt reinforced with rocks
  and concrete also serves (p.186).
- **Weight**: castable weighs about "86lb per cubic foot (39kg per cubic meter)" (p.59, read by
  the user, 2026-10-29). 86 lb is 39 kg, so the metric is per cubic foot; per cubic metre it would
  be 1378 kg (derived) (Flagged).
- The inside stays smooth (from the plastic) until the first firing, then looks like coarse brick
  (p.59, Read).
- No expansion joints in his cast domes (p.60; §2).
- **Durability** (p.116–117, Read): the K-26 LI castable domes of one of his wood kilns lasted
  25 years and over 1500 h at cone 11+. Their surface fused under heat and ash; they have expansion
  cracks but are intact. The hard brick at the flues slagged, spalled and melted first.

## 5. Ceramic fiber

### Materials

- **The fiber** (p.36, Read): normal use limit 2300 °F (1260 °C) to 2600 °F (1426 °C); melts at
  3200 °F (1760 °C); fibers average 4–10 in long. Potters' blanket, board and modules are rated
  2300–2600 °F. Kaowool (2300 °F), %: Al₂O₃ 45; SiO₂ 50–55; Fe₂O₃ 1.0; TiO₂ 1.7; MgO trace; CaO
  0.1; alkalis as Na₂O 0.2; B₂O₃ 0.08; leachable chlorides 1–2.
- **Blanket** (p.36, Read): 3, 4, 6 and 8 lb/ft³; 2 and 4 ft wide, 25 ft long; ¼, ½, 1, 1½ and
  2 in (6, 13, 25, 38, 50 mm) thick. It insulates efficiently to 2600 °F (1426 °C).

### Construction

- **Layers above 2200 °F** (p.64, Read): 1–2 in back-up block, 2 in of 4–6 lb 2200 °F blanket, 2 in
  of 8 lb high-temperature blanket on the hot face. Fig. 2-60 shows, from the hot face: 2600 °F
  8 lb blanket, 2200 °F 4 or 6 lb blanket, 1200 °F back-up board, metal shell (p.62).
  - **Back-up block** (p.64): rated 900–1200 °F, 12–15 lb; rigid, with linear shrinkage "of less
    than" 1 % (read by the user, 2026-10-29); 1, 2 and 4 in thick, 24 in wide, 36 or 48 in long;
    held on the studs with speed clips.
  - **Middle layer** (p.64): blanket or board 4–6 lb/ft³ rated 2200 °F (1204 °C); lower-rated
    blanket could be used, "but I wouldn't go below 1900°F (1038°C)".
  - **Hot face** (p.64–65): two layers of 1 in 8 lb blanket rated 2600 °F (1427 °C). Or a 1 in
    layer of 2600 °F board backed by 1 in of 2600 °F blanket, then the normal middle and back-up;
    board warps and curls at the edges after many firings, so use the full high-temperature stud
    layout even where less is recommended.
  - Each layer is impaled on the studs, staggered over the one beneath, "just as in bricklaying"
    (p.64).
- **Thickness**: 6 in is the most worth paying for in a high-fire kiln, 4 in the minimum (p.65).
  The layers p.65 lists for the 4 in minimum (two 1 in hot-face layers, a 1 in middle layer,
  "another layer of 4–6lb" blanket and 1 in of back-up block) add up to 5 in (derived), unless the
  middle layer and the 4–6 lb layer are the same (Flagged).
- **Joints** (p.65, Read): never hold the hot-face butt joints with a single stud and anchor, and
  never place a stud in the joint between blankets (Fig. 2-64). At high temperature, no labyrinth
  corner joint in the hot-face layers: use an "around the corner" joint. With a board hot face the
  corners are labyrinth joints, but the layer beneath gets a corner joint (Fig. 2-65). Overlapping
  corner joints save studs but thin the wall, and may not suit high temperature (Fig. 2-66).
- **Studs** (p.60–61, Read): lay the welded studs out for the hot-face blanket or board, whatever
  the back-up; the spacing is set by the temperature. Walls need fewer studs per ft² than roofs;
  the higher the firing temperature, the more studs. Stud to the edge of a blanket or board 7 cm
  (2¾ in) in every case. Studs may screw onto a backing instead of being welded.

- **Door seals** (p.66–67, p.175, Read): blanket can't take abrasion, so the closing movement must
  be perpendicular to the seal (a hinged door is easiest; a sliding or guillotine door only if the
  last cinching movement is perpendicular). Fiber has no resilience. A seal near the hot face
  shrinks (0.5 % at high temperature) and opens; it is then over-tightened until destroyed, after
  which the door frame oxidizes and warps. Figs 2-67 and 2-68 mark a 1 in minimum frame gap and a
  "1st firing shrinkage 5%" that, with compression, destroys the seal. Rules: (1) make the sealing
  area replaceable and separate from the door's hot face; (2) put the seal well away from any hot
  face, where it is cooler and shrinks less; (3) fit a doorstop so the seal can't be
  over-compressed. Permanent fiber door and jamb seals are hard to get: design them for easy
  replacement once fully compressed (p.175).
- **Fiber box kilns** (p.148, Read): a suspended flat fiber roof fits down onto three side walls
  and hangs from the corner legs; expanded metal mesh inside the frame backs the fiber walls and
  ceiling; the hearth and the first 15 in of the chamber are 2600 IFB. For his own fiber kiln he
  would use folded-blanket or compressed-module construction (p.175).

## 6. Kiln shape and design principles

- **Before designing** (p.73, Read): decide the kind of kiln (updraft, downdraft, cross-draft,
  circular dome or salt), its size, the clay, the atmosphere (chamber shape follows oxidation,
  reduction or middle-fire; burners and dampers matter), the fuel and the location. A wood kiln in
  a city is "foolish"; propane, butane and electricity work almost anywhere; natural gas in cities
  (check the supply at the site); wood, coal or coke and oil in the country.
- **Siting** (p.74, Read): each place "self-designs" the kiln: no wood kiln in a garage, no anagama
  in the suburbs. Check the building codes before spending money.
- **Design around the shelves** (Read): design the kiln around one of the standard shelf sizes
  (p.74); lay the shelves out on the ground first and build the plan round them (p.137); keep to
  brick-module dimensions even if they look big (p.142).
- **Principle 1: the cube** (p.74–75, Read). A cube is the best all-purpose shape. For an updraft
  put the arch on top of the cube (the most stacking space; the arch volume collects the flue
  gas); for a downdraft, put the arch within the cube. Raising the height at a fixed width makes the
  firing less even. His updraft test kilns with floor burners showed it (p.74):
  - a 2 × 3 ft base with 5 ft-plus of stacking height fired ½ to 1 cone apart top to bottom,
    whatever the schedule; cut to 4 ft high, it was "perfectly even";
  - a 3 × 3 × 5 ft setting fired ½ to 1 cone apart; widened to 4 × 4 × 5 ft, it evened out
    perfectly.
  - Equal height and width matters most with floor-level burners, and the same holds for downdraft
    and cross-draft kilns (p.74).
  - Length doesn't affect evenness (hence tunnel kilns), but the burner count grows with it. Round
    and dome kilns: diameter and height nearly equal (Fig. 3-4); small downdrafts usually count the
    dome in the height, updraft beehives add it on top. For tall kilns, put burners up the sides
    (p.75).
  - Forced-draft updrafts allow up to 25 % more height than width (p.170; §11).
- **Principle 2: shape follows the heat** (p.75, Read). The flame should follow the arch, not cross
  it at right angles (Figs 3-5, 3-6). It should meet only two right angles in the chamber, usually
  at the firebox inlet flue and bag wall and at the exit flues; more cause uneven heating, hot
  spots and refractory failure. A groundhog (Fig. 3-6) runs hot along the crown and cool along the
  bottom of the back wall, where its refractories fail.
- **Transitions** (p.76, Read): if the design runs against the arch's flow, curve or shape the
  transitions from firebox to chamber and from chamber to chimney flue, in insulating castable or
  brickwork. Still, any shape fires given enough heat and a correct flue-to-chimney ratio, and the
  box is the easiest.
- **Principle 3: the combustion channel** (p.76, Read): at least 4½ in (11.5 cm) between ware and
  wall for gas, usually the length of the wall (as p.151 and p.171 say; §7). Principle 3
  "determines the draft rate"; when in doubt make the grate and chimney larger.
- **Order of design** (p.77, Read): design the firebox or combustion area and the chamber first,
  then match the chimney to them. When the result falls between brick sizes, round **up** to the
  brick size (216 → 243 in²; 270 → 304 in², Fig. 3-9).
- Principles 4–8 (taper, chimney height and size, draft rate, climbing-kiln chimneys) are in §9.
- **Principle 9: build to be altered** (p.81, Read): build the critical parts (flues, grate,
  chimney) bigger rather than smaller, so they can be altered. Base every dimension on the
  9 × 4½ × 2½ in brick or the 9 × 4½ × 3 in one. Make the inlet flues, the chimney entrance flue
  and the chimney height adjustable by adding or knocking out a brick.
- **"The kiln doesn't care what it looks like on the outside"**: the one unavoidable rule is that
  it works and reaches temperature with control (p.118–119, Read).
- **Firing speed** (p.139, Read): the rate of rise makes no difference to most bodies and glazes in
  colour or maturity (8 h against 4 h in his gas updraft; 3½ h with wood). The exceptions: clays
  with excess free silica; a half-hour soak helps saggared or tightly packed settings. Cool slowly,
  especially from 1100 °F (590 °C) down to 200 °F (90 °C).
- The first firing of a new hard-brick kiln may be slow, from moisture in the masonry (p.198,
  Read).

## 7. Gas firing

### Combustion and air

- **Terms** (p.196, Read): stoichiometric (complete) combustion burns all carbon to CO₂, hydrogen
  to H₂O and sulphur to SO₂; any C, H₂ or CO left means incomplete combustion. Kindling temperature
  is where a material begins to burn. Natural gas, propane and butane are the cleanest fuels:
  burned completely they leave little or no visible exhaust.
- **Air** (p.197–198, Read): about 10 ft³ of air per 1000 BTU for any fuel; appliances run 10–20 %
  excess air. Methane needs 2 ft³ of O₂ per ft³; with air taken as 20 % O₂ and 80 % N₂, that is
  10 ft³ of air per ft³ of natural gas, or per 1000 BTU (0.29 kW). Propane needs 25 ft³ of air per
  ft³ (2500 BTU, 0.73 kW) (p.197). Table 8-6's air:gas column disagrees and is Flagged (below).
- **Primary air** (p.198, p.224, Read): forced-air burners at least 60 %; atmospheric burners
  20–60 %; raw-gas burners 20 %; tempered-air burners 100 %. Less primary air gives a longer,
  softer flame.
- **Rate of combustion** depends on how intimately fuel and air mix: gas fastest, oil droplets
  between, coal slowest; wood by its size, surface and hardness (p.197, Read).
- **Air must rise with the fuel** (p.198, Read): once a burner's air is set for its rating, any gas
  gives the same heat at perfect combustion. Gas added without air goes up the chimney unburnt:
  1000 ft³/hr of air burns 100 ft³/hr of gas, and another 20 ft³/hr of gas with no more air is
  wasted.
- **Preheat** (p.198, Read): preheating a natural gas–air mixture to 500 °F (260 °C) raises the
  flame temperature about 200 °F (111 °C).
- **Reduction** (Read): burners should be designed for complete combustion at every setting, and
  reduction got by other means (p.198), by partly damping the exit flues rather than by excess gas
  (p.173). Updraft bottom burners are the exception: they mostly aid reduction by adding excess
  gas (p.172). Reduction started too early (around 1200 °F/649 °C) and held heavily throughout
  gives carbon-cored ware (p.197).

### Burner input

- **Rule 1** (p.226, Checked): IFB kiln about 10,000 BTU per ft³ of interior per hour at peak;
  dense firebrick 16,000 minimum, 19,000 maximum. A burner maker's rule of thumb from Southern
  California kiln makers: 150,000 BTU per 9 ft³ (about 16,700/ft³). He thinks most gas kilns are
  "completely overpowered": he fired a 36 ft³ dense cross-draft on six 36,000 BTU/hr burners in
  12–15 h. Rules 2–3: burners = total ÷ rating; each burner's share = total ÷ count.
- **The IFB figure is a range, and a minimum** (p.226–228, Read): the worked example uses "minimum
  figures of 7000BTU" (p.228), so Rule 1 for IFB runs 7,000–10,000 BTU/ft³/hr. His rules are "low
  compared to most. Consider mine the minimum requirements needed for a gas kiln" (p.228). The
  36 ft³ dense cross-draft above ran at 6,000 BTU/ft³/hr (derived), under his own dense minimum, and
  still reached temperature. A 36 ft³ IFB updraft "will have a minimum of 10 such burners" and
  fires easily in about 8 h on the same gas, which is 10,000 BTU/ft³/hr (derived). The only
  advantage of overpowering a kiln is a shorter firing (p.226).
- **Rule 5** (p.227, Read): gas per burner = total ft³/hr ÷ number of burners, or read it off the
  gas-flow chart.
- **Peak efficiency** comes only at the last turn-up. More pressure or gas than the burner's peak
  adds flue gas, not heat. With overpowered or too many burners, peak efficiency isn't needed to
  reach temperature (p.228, Read).
- **Rule 4, gas per hour per ft³ of kiln** (p.227, Read). These are 10,000–7,000 (IFB) and 16,000
  (firebrick) BTU/ft³/hr divided by the heating value. Butane's metric is printed "14cu. m"; it is
  0.14.

  | | Natural gas (1100 BTU/ft³) | Propane (2550) | Butane (3204) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | IFB kiln | 9.1–6.4 ft³ | 3.9–2.8 ft³ | 3.1–2.2 ft³ |
  | Firebrick kiln | 14.5 ft³ | 6.3 ft³ | 5 ft³ |

- **Worked example** (p.227–228, Read): a 20 ft³ IFB cross-draft on natural gas, "maximum
  figures": 20 × 10,000 = 200,000 BTU/hr on four 50,000 BTU/hr burners; Rule 4 gives 182 ft³/hr,
  45 per burner; at 8 in. WC the gas-flow chart gives orifice #34 (the text cites "Fig. 8-44"; the
  chart is Fig. 8-39). A home meter's 175 ft³/hr is 7 short, so no water heater or range can run
  while the kiln fires; at the 7000 BTU minimum the kiln draws 128 ft³/hr, leaving 47, enough for
  a 40-gallon water heater only. Ward's #34 at 8 in. WC is 45,312 BTU/hr, 45 ft³/hr, which agrees
  (derived).

  4.6 oz (p.230, p.232). p.215 prints 0.68 bar per psi (Flagged, it is 0.068); its 6.89 kPa and
  0.0703 kg/cm² are right.
- **High against low pressure** (p.215, Read): high-pressure valves, shut-offs, regulators and
  gauges cost much more to buy and maintain. A high-pressure small orifice and a low-pressure large
  orifice reach the same temperature. Don't convert high-pressure equipment to low pressure or the
  reverse: the burners become "extremely inefficient". The higher the pressure, the smaller the
  orifice (p.221).

### Orifices

- **Orifice equation** (p.216, Read): Q = 1658.5 × K × A × √(ΔP/d), Q in ft³/h, A in in², ΔP in
  in. w.c., d the gas's specific gravity, K 0.4–1.3 (0.80–0.85 for good atmospheric orifices).
  Flow goes as the square root of pressure. So a pressure drop from 16 to 1 in. w.c. cuts the
  capacity to a quarter, and three times the orifice gives three times the capacity: heat input is
  easier to vary by orifice size than by pressure (p.216).
- **Gas-flow chart**, Fig. 8-39 (p.217, Read): Q = 1326 A √(H/G), Q in standard ft³/hr, A in in²,
  H in in. w.c., G = 0.65, natural gas at 1100 BTU/ft³, "based on actual flow tests". Its scales
  run 3–15 in. w.c., 0.3–300 ft³/hr, 400–300,000 BTU/hr and drills #1–80. 1326 is 1658.5 × 0.80
  (derived). Sample: 8 in. w.c. and 30,000 BTU/hr give a #44 drill (p.217); the equation gives
  0.00586 in², which is #44 (derived).
- **Table 8-7, LP gas through standard orifices** (p.218–219, Read; from the *Handbook of
  Butane-Propane Gases*): BTU/hr for drills from .006 in to #10 in four columns: propane (2,500
  BTU/ft³, SG 1.53, 11.0 in. w.c.); butane (3,175, 2.00, 11.0); butane–air (525, 1.16, 5.0);
  butane–air (1,000, 1.31, 7.0). Valid from sea level to 3500 ft. Every column fits the orifice
  equation with K ≈ 0.80; the propane column is about 8.9 million × area (in²) BTU/hr (derived).
- **Normal orifices** (p.235, Read): #28–32 natural gas, #38–42 propane at low pressure. His own
  examples fall outside these: Gas Appliance's mixer takes #45 for propane and #46 for butane at
  11 in. w.c. (p.221); his compound injectors take #18 at 10 in. w.c. (p.224); the 20 ft³ example
  uses #34 on natural gas (p.228). Altitude: §10.
- **Propane through a natural-gas orifice** (p.221, Read): he kept a burner's natural-gas orifice
  (#32), cut the tank pressure below 1 psi and ran at most 4 in. w.c. at the kiln. The flame was
  longer and the temperature better spread. He doesn't recommend it: without a tank regulator that
  goes below 1 psi, raw gas could be forced through the kiln, and the firing costs more.

### Ward's method

- **Ward's tables** (Tables 8-11 to 8-14, pp.230–233, Read and Checked): BTU/hr = pressure
  function × orifice function. Basis: natural gas 1000 BTU/ft³, SG 0.64, orifice coefficient
  0.80, ±5 %. Treat the figures as "a very close estimate, not an exact reference"; the pressure
  is the regulated line (manifold) pressure; don't trust flow gauges, which read differential
  pressure; don't fit an orifice larger than the burner maker recommends (p.230, p.232).
  - Natural gas pressure function = 1.25 × √(in. w.c.) (Table 8-11, p.230): 4 in. 2.5000; 8 in.
    3.5355; 11 in. 4.1458; 1 psi 6.5788; 5 psi 14.7107; 10 psi 20.8041; 25 psi 32.8942. Every row
    from 4 to 27 in. w.c. and from 2 to 25 psi fits (derived).
  - Propane pressure function (Table 8-13, p.232): 4 in. 1.6211; 8 in. 2.2926; 11 in. 2.6884; 1 psi
    4.2661; 5 psi 9.5392; 10 psi 13.4906; 25 psi 21.3306. It is 0.8106 × √(in. w.c.) throughout
    (derived).
  - Orifice function is proportional to the drill's area: about 1.324 × 10⁶ × area for natural gas
    (Table 8-12, p.231, #80 to #1, letter drills and fractions to ½ in), 3.31 × 10⁶ × area for
    propane (Table 8-14, p.233).
  - The products are 1.655 × 10⁶ A √P (natural gas) and 2.683 × 10⁶ A √P (propane) BTU/hr, which is
    the orifice equation with K = 0.80 at 1000 BTU/ft³, SG 0.64 and 2500 BTU/ft³, SG about 1.52
    (derived). Table 8-7 gives propane at 11 in. w.c. as 2.682 × 10⁶ A √P, within 0.1 % (derived).
    So Ward's tables, Table 8-7 and Fig. 8-39 are one equation; the engine can use it and keep the
    tables as tests.
  - Ward's propane page reprints the natural-gas basis word for word ("1000 BTU ... specific
    gravity of 64"); its numbers fit 2500 BTU/ft³ (p.232, Flagged).
  - Misprints in Tables 8-12 and 8-14 are listed in §21. The worst: 31/64 and ½ in at the foot of
    the propane table (244,013 and 260,034) are the natural-gas values copied from Table 8-12, and
    the #13 and 3/16 in rows have their areas swapped in both tables.
  - **Worked examples** (p.230, p.232, Read), usable as tests: natural gas, #38 at 7 in. w.c. is
    10,711.16 × 3.3072 = 35,423.95 BTU/hr; for 125,000 BTU at 7 in., 125,000 ÷ 3.3072 = 37,796.32,
    so #11 (37,932.60); in Atlanta (1050 ft), #28 at 8 in. is 3.5355 × 20,508.76 × 0.9849 =
    71,413.84. Propane, #38 at 11 in. is 26,777.90 × 2.6884 = 71,989.71; for 125,000 BTU at 1 psi,
    125,000 ÷ 4.2661 = 29,300, so #36 (29,492.10); in Atlanta, #38 at 11 in. gives 70,902.66.
  - Altitude factors: §10. Table 8-10 (p.230) lists inch, decimal and mm equivalents from 1/64 to
    ¼ in, for matching orifices to drills (standard values).

### Burners

- **A gas burner** has three parts: mixer, burner and controls (p.215–216, Read).
- **Venturi** (p.216–217, Read): the pressure at the air shutter (P2) must stay below atmospheric.
  The best orifice for a given port gives the highest mixture pressure (P3) consistent with that
  negative P2, which happens when the gas jet touches the throat at a tangent. Limits (p.217,
  p.219, p.220): orifice too large, P3 rises, P2 is less negative, less air, too rich; port too
  small, P2 goes positive and gas blows out of the shutter; port too large, air dilutes the gas;
  gas pressure too low, P2 and P3 fall until the venturi stops working; mixture faster than the
  flame speed, blow-off; slower, flashback.
- **Flashback** usually happens when candling (pre-heating) at too low a pressure. It builds carbon
  at the orifice and can put the flame out, after which raw gas flows into the kiln, dangerous on
  relighting (p.220, Read).
- **Simple atmospheric mixers** are 9–12 in long from orifice to flame-tube tip, with at least
  ½ in between the mixer and the air shutter (p.220, Read).
- **Choosing burners** (p.220, Read): a burner with enough BTU and at least a 6 in. w.c. turn-down
  range is "usually adequate". The more burners for the input, the better the control and
  efficiency. The best burners entrain more primary air. Use burners within their optimum range;
  the maker gives capacity, turn-down, pressure and primary-air percentage.
- **Burner size by kiln type** (p.222, Read): updrafts should use more, smaller burners, which
  spreads the heat; downdrafts and cross-drafts need large burners because they have few inlet
  ports.
- **Flame burning inside the tube at altitude** (p.221, Read): first check the orifice size for the
  altitude; if it is right, cut 1 in off the flame tube at a time until the popping stops. Usually
  2 in is enough.
- **Flame-retention nozzles** (p.221–222, Read): the MR-750's head turns the outer flame back into
  the main cone; it is quiet with excellent turn-down. A "self-piloted" retention nozzle screws onto
  the venturi sleeve for high pressure; its pilot-ring holes sit low to shield the pilot flames
  from cross-drafts, which stops lift-off.
- **Eclipse injectors** (p.222, Read): the needle (adjusting pin) is only for fine adjustment after
  several firings have found the best setting; the orifice should still suit the gas.
- **Ratings printed** (Read): Gas Appliance simple mixer, natural gas #32 → 54,000 BTU/hr at 8 in.
  w.c., propane #45 → 50,000 at 11 in., butane #46 → 57,200 at 11 in. (p.220–221); MR-750 rated
  75,000 BTU, range 17,490 to 78,440 BTU at pressures up to 9.5 in. w.c. (the lower bound is lost in
  the gutter) (p.221); MR-750 and MR-100, 75,000 and 100,000 BTU/hr at 8 in. w.c. (p.222); Eclipse
  standard injector for 530–1200 BTU/ft³ gas, on natural gas at most 7 in. w.c.: 2 in outlet
  83 ft³/hr = 91,300 BTU/hr, 3 in outlet 185 ft³/hr = 203,500 BTU/hr (p.222); Eclipse compound
  injector for 1200–3300 BTU/ft³ gas; a 2 in TR-80 about 214,200 BTU/hr at 10 in. w.c. on a #18
  orifice (p.224), which fits LP, not natural gas (derived); Eclipse TA-53 tempered-air, 20,000 to
  100,000 BTU/hr on natural gas at 5 in. w.c. (p.224); Rohde pot burner, kilns to 2552 °F
  (1400 °C), 20 kW = 68,242 BTU, 0 to 1.5 bar of propane (p.223).
- **Tempered-air burners** (p.224–225, Read): 100 % primary air, no backfire, better economy, wide
  turn-down (TA-53 5:1). Their cost, plumbing and blowers make them "unnecessary for most studio
  kilns". A refractory nose piece in the mixture opening prevents flashback; enclosed block
  burners must have one. With a controller they can fire a set schedule at fixed air:gas ratios.
  Ward Burner Systems sells forced-air systems that fire through a channel along the sides or
  under the floor (p.224).
- **Homemade burners** (p.225–226, Read): 1½–2 in pipe is best; the outlet beyond the elbow is at
  least 3 × the nominal pipe diameter. Size a blower for the total BTU/hr of its burners at 10 ft³
  of air per 1000 BTU; if the blower fails the burner is dangerous, so fit a solenoid shut-off on
  the gas line. For burners of unknown rating, take the number of burner ports from the flue rules
  and, for updrafts, their placement from Ch. 6; Rule 3's BTU per burner gives the orifice (p.226).
  The Alfred burner notes give a 11/64 in orifice at 11 in. w.c. as 202,400 BTU/hr and a #32 as
  "73,600" (?) (p.225); Table 8-7 gives about 206,000 for 11/64 in (agrees) but 94,000 for #32 at
  11 in. (doesn't) (derived). A forced-air 3 in pipe burner "needs 2850–3300 RP[M] 115V 50/60
  C[ycle] ¼ HP motor – 2" × 5" impeller" (p.225).
- **Table 8-8, simple pipe burner** (p.226, Read):

  | Flame tube | Tube length | Manifold | Orifice | Approx. BTU |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 2 in | 7–8 in | 1¼ in | 30 | 40,000 |
  | 1½ in | 7–8 in | 1 in | 32 | 54,000 |
  | 1¼ in | 7–8 in | ¾ in | 39 | 60,000 |

  The #30 and #39 ratings look swapped: at 8 in. w.c. and 1100 BTU/ft³, #30 gives about 66,000,
  #32 about 54,000 and #39 about 40,000 BTU/hr (derived) (Flagged).
- **Mounting** (p.235, Read): never put the orifice above the burner (Fig. 8-58). On right-angle
  burners the arm from the elbow to the nozzle is 3 × the pipe diameter (Fig. 8-59). Don't bury or
  enclose the burner tip or retention nozzle in the port: cool air must flow round it, or the heat
  from the ignition ring, radiation and back pressure oxidizes it and shortens its life.
- **Stuck in reduction** (p.235, Read): if the kiln stays in reduction and won't climb, widen the
  burner ports for more secondary air and check that the exit flue isn't too small. Most burners
  have oversized orifices; fit smaller ones.

### Burner ports and combustion space

- **Burner ports** (Table 8-17, p.236, Checked): tip → port, in: 1½ → 1¾–2; 2 → 2½–3; 2½ → 3–3½;
  3 → 3½–5; 4 → 5–6; 6 → 7–9. The 2 in row prints "½–3"; its cm column (6.3–7.5) shows 2½–3 is
  meant (Flagged). Burners sit ¼–1 in back from the port, more for oxidation (p.235 text,
  Fig. 8-60 on p.236). Updraft floor burners sit ¼–1 in *below* the port (Fig. 8-60).
- **Port shape** (p.235–236, Read): flare sidewall burner ports 15–20° into the chamber where
  possible; updraft floor ports don't need it. Table 8-17's sizes allow for openings made with
  standard hard bricks; in IFB, cut the port nearest the tip size.
- **Smaller ports in other chapters** (Read): a downdraft's inlet port should fit the burner tip, so
  the tip slides through it, the flame passes unrestricted and there is room for secondary air
  (p.151). Updraft floor ports are "just a trifle larger" than the tip (p.173; §11). Table 8-17 is
  roughly ½–2 in more generous (derived); which to use depends on where the burner is.
- **Side-firing portholes** in hard brick or IFB (p.173, Read): 4½ in wide × 5 in tall for one
  burner, or 4½ × 9 in for two burners per port. These are bricklaying sizes; custom burner blocks
  can be any size.
- **Secondary air**: being able to control it is important for atmosphere control (p.173, Read).
- **Combustion space** (p.171, p.151, p.76, Read): at least 4½ in (11.5 cm) around the setting for
  floor burners or 2½–3 in burner tips, usually the length of the wall (p.76, p.171). A 4 in tip
  needs 6 in; tempered-air burner blocks up to 9 in (p.171). Firing from the side or back may need
  7–9 in, depending on the burners: with low-pressure Venturi burners 4½ in is enough, while
  high-pressure burners may need the larger size (p.151). Too small a space can harm the ware
  (p.171).

## 8. Gas supply, piping, venting and safety

### Pipes and manifolds

- **Codes** (p.228, Read): US gas pipe sizing follows the WPOA 1961 Uniform Plumbing Code. In the UK
  all gas work falls under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 and must be done
  by a qualified Gas Safe (Corgi) engineer.
- **Pipe** (p.229, Read): ¾ in pipe minimum to a kiln. Measure the pipe from the meter to the kiln;
  use the next longer length and the next larger capacity in the tables; the pipe size is at the
  top of the column. For several kilns, measure to the furthest and size the main line for all
  the kilns plus 20 %; size each branch by its distance from the main and that kiln's demand.
  Oversize rather than undersize.
- **Table 8-9, equivalent length per standard elbow or tee** (p.229, Read): ½ in 1.6 ft; ¾ in
  2.2 ft; 1 in 2.8 ft; 1¼ in 3.8 ft; 1½ in 4.4 ft; 2 in 5.5 ft.
- **Manifolds** (p.229, Read): the source pipe must carry more than all the burners; each branch is
  sized for its burner orifice plus 20 %, and the branches together should total less than the
  source pipe's capacity. For several kilns the source pipe carries the sum of their BTU ÷
  BTU/ft³. When several kilns fire at once, a regulator for each kiln goes between the tee and its
  valve.
- **One pipe size throughout** (p.234, Read): the usual way for one kiln. Each orifice takes its
  share only while the line's capacity isn't exceeded; past that, the burner farthest from the
  source starves. Burners can be controlled singly or by one valve.
- **Updraft manifold** (p.234–235, Read): the source pipe runs through the control valves into one
  manifold that every burner taps; round its corners to cut friction; up to 20 burners per
  manifold; size it for all the burners plus 20 %. For updrafts up to 72 ft³ he uses 1¼ × 1¼ in or
  1¼ × 2 in channel tubing, bent round the corners (black pipe also works) (p.235).
- **Table 8-15, natural gas (SG 0.65)**, maximum delivery in ft³/hr by length (10–600 ft) and pipe
  size (½–4 in) (p.234, Read). Its column printed "¼" is ¾ in by its values. Sample rows:

### Meters and regulators

- **Meters** (p.227, Read): normal natural-gas pressure is 8 in. w.c.; most home meters give
  175 ft³/hr; the supplier can raise a meter's capacity but not its 8 in. w.c. pressure.
- **LP regulators** (p.221, p.227, p.236–237, Read): an adjustable high-pressure regulator at the
  tank and a low-pressure regulator at the kiln, both with the capacity to fire the kiln. A
  1.25–2 psi high-pressure regulator if the kiln is close to the tank, an adjustable 1–25 psi one
  if it is far away (p.227). A two-stage set-up is recommended: an adjustable tank regulator set at
  5 psi (the Rego LP regulator takes up to 400 psi in and gives 0–30 psi out) plus a regulator at
  the kiln. A single-stage line loses pressure when it is most needed: cold weather, bends, length,
  other use (p.237).
- **Choosing a regulator** (p.236, Read): know the service's inlet pressure, the burners' rated
  pressure, the kiln's peak ft³/hr and the main line's size. Example: meter at 2 psi, burners rated
  8 in. w.c., a 1 in line and 500 ft³/hr: a Maxitrol 325-5 with an E (HO-1) spring, used
  single-stage from pounds down to burner pressure; it takes inlet pressures up to 25 psi, gives
  2 in. w.c. to 3 psi out and passes 675 ft³/hr.
- **Table 8-18, Maxitrol springs** (p.237, Read): D (standard) 2–6 in. w.c.; E (HO-1) 4–12 in. w.c.;
  G (HO-1) 15–30 in. w.c.; H 1–2 psi; J 2–3 psi.
- "Never run house or studio off same regulator as the kiln" (Fig. 8-62, p.237, Read).

### Safety valves and flame safeguards

- **Safety shut-off valve** (p.238–239, Read): a thermocouple safety valve goes first in the line.
  Size it by the gas-line size at the kiln (usually ¾–1¼ in) and the ft³/hr; the regulator's
  output, the safety valve's capacity and the burner on/off valves must all exceed the kiln's
  ft³/hr. To light: hold the red button, light the pilot, release after 60 seconds. Check the
  thermocouple's millivolts and the valve's milliamp dropout range from time to time; use the pilot
  and thermocouple the valve's maker specifies. On a downdraft, each side's burner set-up needs its
  own safety system. A continuous-burning match line with its thermocouple at the end of the line
  is AGA-approved for in-line manifolds (Baso H15DA-1).
- **Commercial flame safeguards** (Fireye UVM and TFM, p.240, Read): the main valve won't open until
  the pilot flame is proved, and if the main burner doesn't light within 12 seconds the gas closes.
  They need a great deal of equipment, and a power loss or a badly set spark gap defeats them. For
  a "normal" 50 ft³ downdraft the parts and installation cost about $3500 / £2188 or more at the
  time of writing. The simplest system is best; the greatest precaution is a kiln fired only by
  someone who understands it and stays for the whole firing.

### Venting

- **Ventilation** (p.240, Read): inadequate ventilation causes more kiln mishaps than any other
  problem. Indoors on natural gas, an updraft needs an exhaust hood covering its entire top, vented
  as directly as possible outside; a downdraft or cross-draft needs a chimney.
- **Flue-gas temperatures** (p.240–241, Read): an updraft's exit-flue gas is about 1750 °F (954 °C)
  at cone 10, and 750 °F four feet above the kiln in the stack (p.240). Fig. 8-67 prints "1750°F
  +/- 50°F" at the top of the kiln, under the hood (p.241).
- **Hood** (p.241–242, Fig. 8-67, Read): the US Uniform Building Code treats the kiln as a
  medium-temperature appliance. Vent an updraft outside through a hood of heavy-gauge sheet metal
  or stainless steel with a proper roof jack: 22 gauge galvanized, lined with the cheapest 2000 °F
  (1100 °C) fiber blanket or board 1 in thick, all joints overlapped, held by nuts and bolts or
  sheet-metal screws on 12 in centres; hood 12–18 in above the kiln top. If the ceiling is low,
  insulate the flue pipe too; the fiber also slows oxidation of the metal and cuts radiant heat
  into the room.
- **Hood flue size**: 1 in² of exhaust per 5000 BTU of kiln input (p.242 text, Read). The drawing
  prints "Size = 150" per 5000 BTU of kiln rating" (p.241, Flagged): take the text. At that rate a
  12 in round flue (113 in²) serves about 565,000 BTU/hr (derived).
- **Roof jack** (p.241–242, Fig. 8-68, Read): the 12 in main exhaust flue runs inside an 18 in liner
  that draws in cooling air; the liner sits on a dead-air box (a 24 × 24 in galvanized sheet-metal
  box nailed to the joists) or the roof jack; a rain cap on the 12 in pipe, a rain shield on the
  18 in; the stack ends about 3 ft above the roof (drawing).
- **Intake air** (p.242, Read): windows, doors, or floor and ceiling vents. If the room gets too hot
  or you can smell exhaust, the intake or exhaust is inadequate; fan-driven roof vents may be
  needed.

### Propane and LP tanks

- **Propane indoors** (p.241, Read): floor ventilation directly outside on at least two opposite
  sides of the kiln; a burner pit below floor level must be vented so propane can run downhill to
  the outside. Never fire propane in a basement (p.238–243).
- **Tanks** (p.242, Read): for a 24–40 ft³ kiln, one 500 US gal tank or two 250 US gal tanks
  manifolded; smaller tanks can be used. Set the tank upright or it delivers liquid instead of
  vapour. Fill to 80 % at most; place it 35 ft from the kiln and the building (p.238–243), and
  downhill and downwind of them (p.242).
- **Freeze-ups** (p.242, Read): they come from moisture; have the dealer add dry methyl alcohol,
  about 1 oz per 20 lb of fuel, or 1 US pint per 100 US gal. On a cold night with the tank under
  30 % full, pressure falls and the tank frosts: pour hot water over it; manifolding two tanks
  cures it; cold climates may need liquid withdrawal. He cites Clifford, *The Practical Guide to
  LP-Gas Utilization* (1969) and Nils Lou, *The Art of Firing* (1998).

### General precautions (p.243, Read)

- Never vent a kiln into exhaust-ventilation ducting.
- Never put the kiln room in a basement or on a floor above ground level when firing gas, oil or
  wood. (This widens the propane rule above.)
- No kilns in classrooms: give them an outside kiln shed.
- Keep the chimney and exhaust vents away from air-conditioning fresh-air intakes on the roof.
- Never bleed propane lines into the kiln before lighting: the explosion blew one kiln's arch and
  door off. Always light with the door cracked and the damper open. After a flame-out, wait with the
  dampers open and spy holes unplugged, then follow the ignition procedure.
- Keep clearance between the kiln and the building's roof, and between the chimney and the roof
  opening, with air space, insulation and flashing where the chimney passes wood. A salt kiln's
  flame through its arch, door and salt ports melted and set fire to a plastic shed roof, and a kit
  kiln under a 7 ft ceiling set its roof alight in the second firing.

## 9. Flues and chimneys

### Exit flues

- **Exit flue, three methods** (p.236, Checked): (1) the total inlet port area; (2) 2½ in² per ft³
  of kiln; (3) 1 in² per 7000 BTU/hr of burner input (1,000,000 BTU/hr → 143 in², about 12×12 in).
  He uses (1) and (3); err large, since a big flue can be bricked down. p.151 agrees: "It is much
  easier to restrict than to increase in size." For flues in an updraft's arch p.174 says the
  opposite (§11).
- **Method 2 against his own kilns** (derived): his starting exit flues for forced-draft IFB
  updrafts fall below 2½ in²/ft³ above about 16 ft³: 16 ft³ → 40½ in² (2.53 in²/ft³); 24 ft³ →
  45 in² (1.9); 50 ft³ → 90 in² (1.8); 100 ft³ → 120–140 in² (1.2–1.4) (p.173–174). Method 3 at
  10,000 BTU/ft³ gives 143 in² for 100 ft³, close to his 120–140. Other kilns: a 250 ft³ coal
  downdraft, 432 in² of floor flues (1.7 in²/ft³, p.131); a 16 ft³ salt kiln, a chimney flue of at
  most 63 in² (3.9, p.136); the 12–16 ft³ fast-fire kiln, 67.5 in² (4.2–5.6, p.139); a 40 ft³ fiber
  downdraft, 54 in² (1.35, p.148). Small kilns run well above 2½ in²/ft³ and large ones below, so
  method 2 suits only small kilns.
- **Natural draft** (p.78, Read): total inlet flue area = exit flue area = chimney cross-section,
  the chimney's entry slightly smaller. Restricted exits slow the flow and retard combustion. The
  chimney's section must be **larger** than the exit flues where they enter it: restrict them with
  a flue collection box behind the chamber and in front of the chimney (in a direct design the
  connection itself restricts the exit). His example enters a 4-brick (162 in²) chimney through a
  3-brick (121.5 in²) entry, 75 % (derived), with 162 in² of inlet flues (four 9 × 4½ in flues).
  A chimney much larger than its flues must be tapered.
- **Pressurized gas** (p.78, Read): inlet area = exit area. Example: ten 2½ in burner ports of
  about 4.9 in² each, 49 in² in all, so about 49 in² of exit flue in the arch: a "safe rule of
  thumb" for updraft gas kilns. In a downdraft or pseudo-downdraft the exit can be blocked down
  later.
- **Forced-draft downdraft or cross-draft** (p.79, Read): inlet flues the size of the burner tips or
  slightly larger (the burner brings primary air; secondary air enters round the burner-port
  hole); usually start brick-sized and reduce, for adjustability. **If wood is also to be burned,
  an auxiliary [firebox or grate; the word is cut off] must be provided.** Exit flues brick-sized
  (reducible) and following the natural-draft flue-to-chimney relationships; the chimney can be at
  least 25 % shorter.
- **Downward-draft gas kilns** (p.151, Read): the exit flue starts equal to the inlet burner-port
  area, then is adjusted smaller. The chimney section starts equal to the inlet-port area
  (Fig. 5-82: "burner port area = chimney area"). Make the exit flue's width equal to the
  chimney's, then restrict it by adding a brick.
- **Standard flue**: one brick on end, 9 × 4½ in, spaced 9 in apart (p.81, p.45), which serves
  "perhaps 80 % of the time" (p.81, Read). Kyoto climbing kilns use 9 × 5, 9 × 6, 7 × 10 and
  9 × 10 in flues to suit odd local bricks, though standard flues 9 in apart would do
  (p.91, p.96–97, Read).

### Chimney height

- **Principle 5** (p.80; restated p.130): 3 ft per ft of downward pull plus 1 ft per 3 ft of
  horizontal pull, measured inside the kiln (the printed "(hp + 3)" means hp ÷ 3). His example: a
  chamber 6 ft to the crown with 7 ft of horizontal pull gets 18 ft + 2 ft 4 in = 20 ft 4 in
  (p.79–80, Read). Fast-fire wood: 3 × (chamber + firebox height) plus the horizontal allowance
  (p.140). The kiln's own figures favour that reading: 3 × (36–40 in chamber + 12½–15 in firebox) is
  about 12–14 ft against the 14 ft chimney printed, while 3 × chamber + firebox gives only 10–11 ft
  (derived).
- **Shorter chimneys for gas** (Read): pressurized gas allows a third or more shorter (p.80);
  downdraft and cross-draft kilns on pressurized gas "at least 25 %" shorter (p.79); forced draft
  about a quarter shorter, or until the draft is 4–5 ft/s (p.130–131); with forced-draft burners
  "as much as 25 %, but there is no certain rule. The only way to determine the correct height is
  to fire the kiln and adjust" (p.131). p.79 and p.80 disagree on the number for the same case;
  both are lower bounds.
- **Gas and wood together** (p.79, read by the user from the book, 2026-10-29; the word is cut off
  at the gutter in the scan): "If a combination of wood is also to be used, then you must provide
  an auxillary [sic] air source." It follows the shorter chimney for pressurized gas: a chimney
  cut down for gas doesn't draw a wood fire's air.
- **Taper** (Principle 4, p.79–80, Read): the taper of a chimney controls the rate of draft. "If
  the chimney seems too tall, it probably is." A tapered chimney can lose about 2 ft (p.80); the
  more it tapers, if the base is large enough, the more height can come off. Tapering can begin
  once the chimney passes the top of the arch (p.80). A modern taper is printed "1 to ¾ or 1⅓ (?)
  to 1" (p.131); early Far Eastern chimneys tapered 2½ to 1 (p.130).
- **Principle 7** (p.80–81, Read): too tall a chimney speeds the gas, heats unevenly, pulls heat out
  and prolongs the firing; too short also prolongs it, because fuel builds up in the firebox
  without enough oxygen drawn in.
- **Natural-draft downdraft proportions** (p.129–131, Read). Fig. 5-2: chamber width = height =
  length (2 m); flue connection ½ the width (1 m); chimney base diameter ½ the width (1 m); chimney
  height 2 × the width (4 m); taper 2½ : 1; the same chimney serves a chamber scaled down to 1.7 m
  (67 in). These proportions give the **minimum** height and Rule 5 the **maximum** (p.130).
  Fig. 5-3, a modern 6 × 6 ft chamber: chimney base at least 12 in in diameter, top 8 in, height
  at least 20 ft; a chamber-to-chimney distance under 3 ft doesn't change it (p.130–131).
  - p.130 says Rule 5 adds only "an extra 1½ft (46cm)" for the chamber-to-chimney distance. That
    can't be reproduced: by Rule 5 a 6.56 ft chamber already needs about 19.7 ft, some 6.5 ft more
    than Fig. 5-2's 13.12 ft (derived) (Flagged).
  - The chimney must be at a right angle to the firebox flues to pull the draft down through the
    middle of the chamber (p.130). This is a proportion of the early kilns, not a hard rule: an
    in-line chimney works on a true-downdraft fast-fire variant (p.146).
- **Downdraft kilns with burners** (Chapter 5): chimney as tall as the kiln, or level with the top
  of the kiln framework, with a ventilation hood and stack above; its section starts at the
  burner-port area (p.151, Read). p.150 applies this to back-wall "downward-draft" kilns, which he
  says are cross-drafts.
- **Updrafts and commercial kilns** (p.80, Read): updraft kilns have no chimneys; most commercial
  cross-draft (pseudo-downdraft) kilns stop the chimney just above the arch and vent out of the
  building.
- **Climbing kilns** (Read): the chimney should reach the line through the chamber tops, the kiln's
  slope, as a guide to its **minimum** height (Principle 8, p.81, Fig. 3-15 at 22°). The Bizen
  kiln's is about three times the kiln's total rise, 25 ft (p.101). Rule 5 alone doesn't cover
  climbing kilns.
- **Groundhogs** (p.111–112, Read): chimney 5–10 ft tall by the text (p.112) or 5–12 ft by
  Fig. 4-85 (p.111), for chambers 12–24 ft long; dug-out fireboxes (about 48 in deep) allow shorter
  chimneys than shallow ones (p.112). These are empirical ranges. Rule 5 gives about 6.7 ft for a
  20 ft horizontal run alone (derived).

### Chimney size and draft

- **Chimney size** (p.79–80, Read): ¼ to ⅕ of the chamber's diameter (Rule 6); seldom under
  9×9 in at the base in natural draft; a 12–16 ft chimney with a 12×12 in base tapers to at least
  9×9 in. Early Far Eastern downdrafts used **½** the chamber's diameter, so one chimney could serve
  more kilns, with a "greatly restricted" entrance flue and a 2½ : 1 taper (p.130). An oversized
  chimney diameter is safer; Rule 6 needs fewer bricks and no taper (p.130). Fig. 5-3's 12 in base
  for a 72 in chamber is ⅙ (derived), so for downdrafts the book spans ⅙ to ½ of the chamber width.
  Small chimneys work: a wood kiln with a chimney "only 9 x 9in." fires well (p.124), as do the
  9 × 9 in chimneys of the fast-fire kiln (p.139–140) and a 40 ft³ fiber gas downdraft (p.148).
- **Draft velocity** (p.79–80, Read): ideal 4–5 ft/s (122–152 cm/s) in the kiln. That rate comes at
  the kiln's most efficient stage, usually after 2000 °F (1093 °C) in cone 10 kilns; early in the
  firing it is very slow, so measure it through the firing. The path is the inside circuit: up the
  front wall, over the arch, down the back wall, through the flues and up the chimney; 45 ft takes
  "about 10 seconds" (Fig. 3-13, p.79). **Oily-rag test**: throw an oily rag into the firebox and
  count the seconds until smoke leaves the chimney; if too slow, taper the chimney (p.79).
- **Dampers** (Read): a climbing kiln's chimney pulls strongly at the start; damping while firing
  the first or second chamber backs up the draft for reduction, opening it gives oxidation; close
  the flue damper at the end for slow, even cooling (p.102). A passive-damper air hole can be built
  into the back of the chimney (p.141). Damper platforms and guides: §11.
- **Measured kilns** with their flues and chimneys (whole kilns in `olsen-kilns.md`): the fast-fire
  kiln pp.139–140 and the Breda bottle kiln pp.167–168 (Checked); Conrad's teardrop p.124, Singh's
  round kiln pp.133–134, Kähler's salt kiln p.136 and Blakebrough's fiber downdraft pp.148–149, now
  read from the page images (Read).

## 10. Altitude

- **Natural draft** (p.81–82, Read): the loss is "very apparent" over 3800 ft (1158 m) in hot
  deserts and at 5000–10,000 ft in mountains. Outside air temperature counts as much as elevation:
  in Aspen, Colorado, air at 72 °F (22 °C) is as thin as at 10,000 ft, so firing is more efficient
  at night. Above about 4000–5000 ft, make the chimney and flues 50 % larger and the chimney at
  least 30 % taller; the grate area is unchanged. Round each increase to "the closest bricklaying
  combination": a 9 in chimney side becomes 13½ in, 9 × 4½ in flues become 13½ × 4½ in (Figs 3-16,
  3-17).
- **Fast-fire wood kiln** (Read): above 4000 ft add 3 ft or more to the chimney and 30 % more area
  (p.140). Its drawings give the high-altitude sizes: exit flue 9 × 7½ in → 13½ × 7½ in; chimney
  9 × 9 in → 13½ × 13½ in; a sixth course on the firebox (109 bricks instead of 90) (p.139–140). The
  same design fired to cone 10 flat in 1 h 55 min at his 6000 ft studio (p.309). The 7:1 grate
  ratio leaves room to adjust for altitude (p.77).
- **Forced-draft updrafts** (p.174, Read): above 5000 ft (1500 m) use equal inlet and exit areas for
  kilns of 40 ft³ and under (instead of a slightly smaller exit), and plan a 20 % larger exit flue
  for larger kilns. The natural-draft 50 % rule doesn't apply to them.
- **Burner orifices** (Read): one drill size smaller per 2000 ft above 5000 ft (p.228, p.235),
  because there is less oxygen per ft³ of air and the air–gas ratio must rise (p.228). Ward: "Do not
  increase orifice size at altitude to compensate unless mechanical air (blower) increases are
  available" (p.230, p.232). The note under Table 8-7, quoted from the *Handbook of Butane-Propane
  Gases*, says the opposite with a lower threshold: the tables hold to 3500 ft; 3500–5000 ft one
  drill size **larger** (about 0.002 in more diameter); 5000–6500 ft two sizes larger (about
  0.004 in); above 6500 ft two to three sizes larger (p.219). The user confirmed the reading:
  "one drill size larger (approximately 0.002" dia.)" (2026-10-29). The book doesn't reconcile
  them (Flagged): the handbook's larger orifice keeps the input up at altitude, where Olsen and
  Ward keep the air–gas ratio; use Olsen's and Ward's rule unless a source explains the
  handbook's.
- **Ward's altitude factors** on burner output (p.230, p.232, Read):

## 11. Updraft kilns

### Contemporary gas updrafts

- **Three requirements** for an efficient forced-draft updraft (p.170, Read):
  - a cube chamber, height equal to width; keeping the width close to the height matters for even
    firing, and the length can be anything, as in tunnel kilns. **The height can be up to 25 %
    greater than the width** without much risk of uneven firing; his 16 ft³ kit is 25 % taller
    than wide and "fires quite evenly";
  - forced draft from natural gas, propane or butane delivered under pressure, low or high;
  - floor inlet burner ports, so the flame flows naturally up through the ware.
- **Too tall** (p.170–171, Read): more than 25 % extra height over a fixed width and length reduces
  evenness in a normal 8–12 h firing; floor burners alone can't then hold an even temperature
  without long soaks.
- **Why updrafts** (p.177, Read): the lowest cost per ft³ to build and fire and the most usable
  stacking space. The design adapts to a hood envelope (the chamber lifted away), a sliding
  envelope (on tracks), a car kiln or a tunnel kiln. Designed and built correctly, it fires
  extremely evenly with good atmosphere control.
- **The small cube kiln** (p.171, Read): printed as "a 2, 3 or 4cu. (0.06, 0.08, or 0.11cu. m) cube
  (in terms of the amount of stacking space)" with "a 1½in. (4cm)-rise number 1 arch" (presumably
  1½ in per ft, the No.1 featheredge). It takes at most six atmospheric burners plus one optional
  bottom burner, each 35,000–54,000 BTU/hr (printed "10.26 to 15.83Kwh"), round the perimeter; or
  three per side with left and right burner channels. Six burners give 210,000–324,000 BTU/hr,
  about 10,000 BTU/ft³ at 21–32 ft³ (derived), and Fig. 6-63 draws a cube 3–4 ft a side, so "2, 3
  or 4cu." most likely means a 2, 3 or 4 ft cube, not 2–4 ft³ (Flagged).
- **Side channels** (p.171, Read): many updrafts fire through side channels because the burner and
  safety system are then easier to configure to meet safety standards.
- **Burner placement** (p.171–172, Read):
  - perimeter burners at least 6 in and at most 12 in from the corners, the rest 9–12 in apart
    (p.171 text);
  - Fig. 6-63's caption prints "9 to 12in. (23 to 30.5cm) from the corners, and 12 to 24in.
    (30.5 to 61cm) apart", and its drawing shows 9–12 in from the wall and 9–12 in apart,
    "depending on the number of burners distance can be less (6–12")" (p.172, Flagged: the caption
    disagrees with the text);
  - side-channel burners 1–6 in from the corners, then 9–12 in apart along the channel on both
    sides; a back-wall circulation space of 2–4½ in (p.171, Fig. 6-63). The plans mark "4½" Around",
    perimeter burners at 10, 12 and 10 in, and side burners in 4½ in channels at 6, 12, 12 and 6 in.
  - Leave space behind and in front of the shelves for circulation (p.171).
- **Large updrafts** (p.171–172, Read): perimeter burners are recommended. A 500 ft³ car kiln has
  32 atmospheric burners, ten on each side and six at each end, with a 9 in combustion width and
  standard port spacing (p.170–172). A 36 ft³ IFB updraft needs at least ten 36,000 BTU/hr burners
  (p.226; §7).
- **Bottom burners** (p.172, Read): they can raise the bottom temperature but are mostly used to aid
  reduction by adding excess gas. Keep **at least 2½ in (6.3 cm) between the setter shelf and the
  floor**. Shelf supports: a rectangular "S" layout with a single bottom burner suits 12 × 24 or
  12 × 18 in shelves; use an "X" layout for a perimeter or channel combustion area. Never enclose
  bottom burners with shelf supports: back pressure cuts circulation and damages the burner tips.
- **Floor burner ports** (p.173, Fig. 6-67, Read): make the port "just a trifle larger" than the
  burner tip or injector nozzle, usually 2–3 in in diameter; a 2½ in tip gets a 2½–2¾ in port.
  With 3 in tips the inlet flues are also 3 in; if they come out bigger, re-evaluate the
  combustion-area width. Square holes are common because they fit the brick coursing, but fit
  them to the burner as closely as possible. The tip sits at least ¼ in below the floor; most
  floors are 4½–5 in thick. Table 8-17's ports are larger (§7).
- **Exit flues** (p.173–174, Checked on p.174): starting sizes 16 ft³ of stacking → 40½ in²
  (p.173, Read); 24 ft³ → 45 in² (p.173–174, Read); 50 ft³ → 90 in²; 100 ft³ → 120–140 in².
  Exit equal to or slightly under the inlet area up to 36 ft³ (p.174); about 20 % more from
  50 ft³. p.173 gives the same rule as "more than adequate" for kilns of 40 ft³ or less, and the
  p.174 altitude rule also uses 40 ft³: the limit is 36 or 40 ft³ depending on the sentence.
  - His kits (p.174, Read): 24 ft³, inlet 50 in² (eight perimeter and two bottom burners), exit
    45 in²; a 50 in² exit "can be used, but the kiln would have to use the damper to block the extra
    area". 100 ft³, inlet 100 in², exit 120 minimum to 140 in². Kilns over 50 ft³, with more input,
    "require larger exit flues in order to fire evenly, quickly and efficiently in oxidation or
    reduction".
  - **Start small in an arch** (p.173–174, Read): flues in the arch "are easily made bigger, but
    are difficult to make smaller even when using IFB refractories", so start slightly smaller than
    or equal to the inlet area, with the option to enlarge; up to 40 ft³ he makes them slightly
    small and test-fires. Elsewhere the book says to start large and restrict (p.151, p.236).
  - **Testing** (p.173, Read): after bisque, back off the damper: the kiln should go oxidizing at
    once. If exit-flue flames remain with the damper fully open at about cone 8, check for too much
    gas pressure; if the pressure is right, enlarge the exit flue when the kiln is cold.
  - **Bricking the flue holes** (p.173, Fig. 6-68, Read): the bricklaying decides between one exit
    flue and several. No free-floating bricks along the key-brick flue-hole row: they drop out.
- **Dampers** (p.174, p.176, Read): the damper platform and seal matter in both firing and cooling.
  Large exit flues need a good damper system. An arch needs a flat platform built round the flue
  holes for the damper seal; a flat roof already has one. Make all areas easily changeable
  (p.174). A metal slide guides a kiln-shelf damper over the platform, which is cut-and-fitted
  brick or dense castable cast in a frame round cardboard flue forms, levelled and cured under a
  wet cloth and plastic; the shelf should not grind on the platform but should close tight (p.176).
- **Laying order** (p.176, Read): lay the floor first. With a butt door, lay the interior next,
  close and bolt the door, and butt the seal bricks to it; lay the arch last and cover it with an
  insulation blanket or a vermiculite mix. With an inset door, lay the arch first and sand the door
  to fit the arc. Featheredge firebricks form the skewbacks of No.1 arches.
- **Flat roof against arch** (p.177, Read): a flat roof is easier to build, its height is
  adjustable and it reflects heat like the flat walls, but it needs its own frame, bolted or
  welded; an arch needs a frame built to its featheredge rows before it is laid. Construction is "a
  draw". On heat distribution he "would bet that the arch would win": not for any "lens" effect,
  but because the arch gives a more natural, aerodynamic flow over the top and down round the kiln.
  A flat roof makes a hot spot and stress point where the flame strikes it: spalling and slagging
  in wood kilns, and in gas kilns where marginal lower-temperature IFB takes the flame. Raising an
  adjustable roof too far harms the firing (compare the 25 % rule).
- His kits use no fiber seals (p.175; §5). Indoor venting: §8.

### Primitive and historic updrafts (Ch. 6)

- **Umbrella floors** (p.154, Read): shards fill the gaps between the spokes of the floor, to absorb
  direct flame shock and stop pots falling through.
- **A gentle start**: a slow build-up of coals in a pit under the chamber floor brings the chamber
  gradually to 500 °F (260 °C), for the safety of the ware (p.155, Read).
- **Shallow floor domes**: a brick floor dome with a very shallow rise, to a 12 in keystone, becomes
  a problem once the bricks start to fracture (p.156–157, Read).
- **Cube proportions** (p.158, Read): chamber, sub-chamber and firebox as three equal cubes; "A
  kiln-chamber cube with a domed roof is a perfectly proportioned" chamber.
- **Dome flues** (p.158, p.168, Read): the corner flues in a dome are exit and temperature-control
  flues. Closing selected flues channels heat to the open ones; over a cold area, pull a plug to
  draw the flame there. Evenness can be controlled by damping and opening the dome exit flues.
- **Floor arches** (p.160–161, Read): firebox ring arches 9 in apart, spanning the chamber, carry a
  floor bearing 20 tons (18 t) of ware. The arch-top bricks and a first layer of tiles at right
  angles to the floor bricks let the gases circulate through the whole setting.
- **Graded setting in an updraught tunnel kiln** (p.164, Read): grade the setter-tile spacing over
  the flue channels from tight at the firebox end to open at the far end: tight for the first 4 ft,
  then "about  in. (1cm)" apart (the fraction is missing in print) for the next 6 ft, then ¾ in
  graduating to 1¼ in, the last 8 in completely open. Greenware tiles shrink, so every gap grows
  about 10 % by the end. Close the exit-flue rows nearest the firebox, damper the next and open the
  far rows, to pull the flame to the far end.
- **Böttger's improvements** (about 1700, p.166, Read): refractory bricks for higher temperatures; a
  better firebox with cast-iron grates; coal and coke as fuel; and a frustum-of-a-cone chimney,
  which "increases the draft and fuel-burning capacity".
- **Bottle-kiln setting** (p.168, Read): stack the pots off the floor, leaving a dead-air space
  between the flues and the setting; a 2 ft (61 cm) bagwall inside the perimeter inlet flues
  baffles the direct flame.
- **Two-storey bottle kilns** (p.168, Fig. 6-54, Read): a centre flue off the firebox diverts part
  of the flame to the middle of the chamber to even the temperature; the dome is extremely thick,
  2 ft or more. Their shortcomings (p.169): huge walls and heavy external iron bracing; much fuel
  spent heating brickwork; costly masonry; firing evenly "impossible"; a very long firing and
  cooling cycle.
- Firebox, ash pit and stoking rules from these kilns are in §14.

## 12. Downdraft and downward-draft kilns

- **Definition** (p.129, Read): the draft starts at the inlet flues, circulates in the chamber,
  passes down through floor exit flues and out the chimney (Fig. 5-1). The advantages: inherently
  even temperature; control of temperature distribution and atmosphere; economical fuel use; and
  it can be enlarged greatly while keeping a small kiln's firing behaviour.
- **True and "downward" draft** (p.150, Read): a true downdraft sends the gases through the floor
  into a collection channel and up the chimney. Box and sprung-arch kilns whose exit flue is at
  floor level in the back wall are "pseudo" downdrafts; Olsen calls them "downward draft" and says
  they are "basically a crossdraft kiln".
- **Proportions** (p.129–130, Read): the height-to-width ratio is very important for even firing
  (Ch. 3, Rule 1); use proportional dimensions or multiples (Fig. 5-2); follow all the Chapter 3
  rules. Round domed downdrafts follow the same design and proportions as square or rectangular
  ones (p.133). Chimney proportions: §9.
- **Floor flues as practised** (p.131–132, Read): a 250 ft³ coal downdraft has 432 in² of floor
  exit flues (twelve 6 × 6 in) and about 500 in² of inlet flues from its fireboxes, "a bit larger",
  equalized by dampening the air supply when firing. His proposed redesign makes the chimney flue
  equal to the total floor exit flues (24 × 18 = 432 in²) and the chimney 18 × 18 in (324 in²,
  75 %, derived) at the top. Flue channels under the floor may be the same width as the floor
  flues (p.132).
- **Heat path** (p.151, Read): heat enters at floor level, follows the wall to the top, then comes
  down to the back-wall exit flue at floor level; the same holds for cross-draft kilns.
- **Bagwalls for side firing** (p.151, Figs 5-83, 5-84, Read): a side-fired kiln needs a bagwall to
  turn the flame up the vertical combustion channel. It can be a built-up floor, a free-standing
  solid bagwall the full length, or a full-length row of rowlock or soldier bricks that also
  carries the shelves; with that row the first shelf sits at 4½ or 9 in.
- **Back firing** (p.151, Read): firing down the length of the channel from the back may need
  deflection bricks to send the flame evenly upward; long kilns may need burners at both ends.
- **Shelf spacing** (p.151, Fig. 5-82, Read): 1–2 in between the shelves and the back wall, 1 in
  between shelves, and a larger space in front (3–4 in in the figure). Set too far forward, the
  front fires cold; shelves butted against the back wall leave the back cold. A staggered setting
  isn't needed if the back spacing is right. The fast-fire kiln uses a 4 in front space, which
  matters less with equally spaced floor burners left and right.
- **Walls and bag walls** (Read): bowed walls turn the circular downward flame to the centre sooner
  than straight walls (p.132). Bag walls run "just a few feet shorter than the chamber length" and
  act as baffles that send the flame evenly into the chamber; the firebox opening into the chamber
  must be large enough for the bag wall to work (p.132). A freestanding bag wall "has a tendency to
  collapse": instead, notch the chamber wall itself at each firebox (9 in deep, 2 ft wide, 3 ft
  long, with a 15 × 24 in firebox flue entrance), tapering into the dome, which also starts the
  circular downward flame; this works in square or rectangular downdrafts too (p.134–135). A round
  kiln gets extra brick bracing between its fireboxes (p.134).
- **Stacking** (p.132, p.135, Read): bottom shelves at least 4½ in off the floor; shelves gapped and
  staggered about 2 in apart so the draft reaches the floor flues. Too tight a setting gives uneven
  temperature and a hot firebox. Bottom saggars are fluted and set about 1 in or more apart,
  wedged against each other. Coal, coke or oil need saggars against ash and sulphurous gases; a
  tightly stacked round downdraft left 6–8 in under the dome and about 12 in round the saggars up
  to the skewbacks (p.135).
- **Side-fired burners for a rotating flame** (p.136–137, Read): with opposing side burners a
  clockwise flame develops in the upper half of the chamber, and "This rolling action gives even
  temperature distribution". The front burners must be high above the exit flue and the back
  burners lower, placed midway between the opposing ports. In a 16 ft³ salt kiln the front burners
  sit 18 and 39 in above the floor and the back ones 9 and 28 in, forced air/gas burners in
  super-duty port blocks (K28 dense high-alumina) (p.136). Its floor slab stands 4½ in clear of the
  walls, so the gas passes round its edge into 4½ × 9 in collection channels below; its chimney flue
  is at most 9 × 7 in and the chimney 10–12 ft tall (p.136).
- **Envelope kiln sizing** (p.148–149, Read): start from a cube and adjust it to the shelves and
  burner channels. Example: 42 × 54 × about 52 in inside for two 24 × 24 in shelves, 40 ft³ of
  stacking (68 ft³ gross, derived); two 4½ in flue channels, 9 in apart and 6 in deep, into a
  9 × 6 in chimney flue and a 9 × 9 in chimney; eight floor exit flues; two 4½ × 6 in burner ports
  in the back wall firing down 9 in side-burner channels; four venturi compound injector burners,
  each with its own safety and ignition system.

### Fast-fire wood kilns (Ch. 5)

- **Performance** (p.139, p.143, Read): a 12–16 ft³ fast-fire wood downdraft reaches cone 10 in
  3½ h on a quarter cord of wood of any kind; "less than a quarter of a cord of wood for a 3½ to
  4-hour firing" (p.143). A kiln built all of hard brick takes longer: "A 40cu. ft (1.13cu. m) kiln
  will have an average firing time of 12 to 14 hours" (p.143). A logged firing took 4 h 45 min to
  cone 10, "slow"; the same design fired to cone 10 flat in 1 h 55 min at 6000 ft (p.309).
- **Scaling** (p.139, p.142, Read): the family runs 12–45 ft³; the design "can be enlarged to almost
  any size between 12 and 120cu. ft" by enlarging the cube with the inlet flues, exit flue and
  chimney. It can be built of IFB or hard brick and stacked loose or tight.
- **Construction** (p.139–141, Read): chamber and arch of 2300 °F (1260 °C) IFB, one brick (4½ in)
  thick, backed by flexboard or another rigid board, or sheet metal; featheredge skewbacks and a
  bonded arch of No.1 arch bricks with straights, butting the back wall, which is laid past the
  arch; the door rowlocks also go past the arch. Two fireboxes under the chamber in an "S" plan,
  built of straight hard bricks, each 13½ in wide; inlet flues 4½ in × the firebox width at the far
  end of each firebox (p.141). The firebox is spanned with kiln shelves, bricks "or even a
  castable" (p.141), or with butted rowlocks, circle bricks, arch bricks or straights on a form
  (p.145). The chimney platform is built level with the firebox top and the walls are tied to the
  chimney to form the exit flue (p.140–141). Its drawings: chamber 27 in wide, 36–40 in to the
  springline; exit flue 9 × 7½ in; chimney 9 × 9 in × 14 ft; firebox base 45 × 40½ in; inlet flues
  4½ × 13½ in; pull-out grate 24–27 in long, 12–13 in wide, 7–8 in tall; 90 low-duty firebricks for
  the fireboxes; 40 bricks for the chimney foundation (p.139–140). The text says the firebox is "6
  bricks tall"; Fig. 5-39 draws five courses and adds a sixth only "for high altitude" (p.140,
  Flagged); five courses (12½ in) match p.199.
- **Bag wall and setting** (p.141–142, Read): a row of rowlock bricks from back to front along the
  edge of the inlet flues on each side is both bag wall and first-shelf support, and makes the flue
  channel under it; "Two rows high is sufficient for the bagwall"; keep it tight along the
  inlet-flue area and more open away from it. Lay the shelves out leaving 4½ in on both sides (over
  the inlet flues) and in front, with 1 in gaps between shelves and at the back.
- **Door and chimney** (p.141, Read): brick the door up with stretchers (4½ in) or rowlocks (2½ in),
  with pull-out cone-hole plugs and an empty hole about 2½ × 2½ in at the top centre. A passive
  damper air hole can go in the back of the chimney.
- **Other fuels** (p.142, Read): the design can burn gas or oil: oil drip plates or forced oil
  burners in the firebox area; or gas ports 4½ × 4½ in at floor level in the side walls, made with
  removable bricks, with the inlet flues blocked and the firebox mouth bricked up and mud-sealed.
  The setting then acts as bag wall and flame deflector; size the burners by Chapter 8.
- **Firing** (p.142–143, Read): fire one firebox until a coal bed forms, then start the other, at
  about 1100 °F (590 °C), and alternate; never full-stoke both at once. Fill the grate half full and
  let it burn down; after an hour or so each firebox takes only four to six kindling-size pieces
  about every three minutes, the other side one or two meanwhile. Keep a spy hole open at all
  times, preferably at the top. In the first firing at one workshop the damper went a quarter open
  at 1250 °F, half at 1400 °F and three quarters at 1820 °F (Table 5-2, p.142). Stoke when the flame
  sucks back into the kiln and the chimney has cleared. "Critical temperatures are around 1100°F
  (590°C) and 1994°F (1090°C), where care must be taken not to choke the kiln." If the kiln loses
  temperature, rake and clear coals out of the firebox and beat the wood down on the grate for more
  oxygen. Never let the coals under the grate build up to touch it, especially at the inlet flue.
  Soak at the final cone to even out (p.143).
- **Results** (p.145, Read): a fast-fire salt kiln with 9 in walls fired with "about half a cone's
  difference between the top and bottom" on its first firing. Raising a kiln on a platform of
  building blocks (cores filled with sand) puts the fireboxes at sitting height for stoking (p.143).
- **Variants** (p.146–147, Read): one true-downdraft variant leaves the space between the two
  firebox channels open as the exit-flue channel, with the chimney in line with the fireboxes
  (offset clear of the firebox mouth) and widely spaced 3 in ceramic tubes as grate bars in the
  middle of each firebox (p.146). The Phoenix type has a Roman-arched firebox under the chamber
  floor, a single inlet flue at the front of the chamber, and an exit flue at floor level at the
  back into a chimney that shares the chamber's back wall (p.147).

## 13. Cross-draft, climbing, tunnel and multi-directional kilns

- **Cross-draft** (p.83, Read): the flame moves from inlet flues on one side of the chamber to exit
  flues on the opposite side. The four common types are single-chamber, chamber, climbing tube and
  flat tube (Fig. 4-1).
- **Anagama** (p.83–84, p.122, p.178, Read): the original was a hole dug into a clay bank, tapered
  back into a chimney flue, shaped, dried and bisqued in place by weeks of increasing fire. Plan an
  anagama-style kiln as one third firebox and two thirds stacking (p.122, Fig. 4-129). An anagama
  can be hard to fire evenly, depending on the tube's length and its side stoking, and is easily
  tipped into reduction and choked (p.178).
- **Slopes** (Read): a Korean climbing kiln's text says "A 20% slope was used, which is standard for
  most kilns of this style" (p.108), but its drawing prints "20° SLOPE" (p.107); 20 % is about
  11.3° and 20° about 36 % (derived) (Flagged). Other slopes: an anagama drawn at 25° (Fig. 4-5,
  p.84); a climbing kiln drawn at 22° (Fig. 3-15, p.81); a Kyoto-style kiln at "3 TO 1 SLOPE",
  about 18° (derived) (Fig. 4-36, p.95); the Bizen kiln a 5 in rise per 12 in, about 22.6°
  (derived) (p.101); a chamber kiln firebox drawing "slope 33%" (p.202). A firebox for slab wood
  must be spacious enough for 3–5 ft slabs (p.108).
- **Black (carbonized) ware** (p.108, Read): at temperature, stuff the firebox with wood, brick up
  the door and seal it with dirt; close the chimney flues and cover them with dirt; find leaks with
  a burning newspaper and plug them with mud. The kiln must be sealed tight to cool in a smoky,
  reducing atmosphere.

### Tube and climbing chamber kilns

- **Long preheat** (p.86, Read): about 36 h of firebox stoking dries the kiln and ware and starts
  the draft before side stoking begins.
- **Side stoking** (p.87, Read): leave a 9 in fire channel (himichi) between every second or third
  stoke hole. Stoke both sides until flame shows two or three holes further on, let it die down,
  and repeat. Wood entering cools the kiln, so the interval matters: too long between stokings and
  the temperature stalls.
- **Chamber proportions and atmosphere** (p.91–92, Read): taller, narrower chambers (Kyoto, 4 ft
  wide × 6 ft tall) spread heat better and give more control over atmosphere; chambers about as
  wide as they are tall ("other climbing kilns", 6 × 6 ft) run more oxidizing and reduce less
  easily. Offset-peak (double-radius) arches, peak toward the back, congest the flow and help
  reduction; nearly single-radius arches release it for oxidation and middle-fire.
- **Stoking for atmosphere** (p.92, Read): for reduction stoke at quick intervals and keep the coals
  stacked high in the firebox; for oxidation let each stoking burn down completely before the
  next. p.94 (read by the user, 2026-10-29): "In the case of middle to oxidation firing, stoking
  must precede each complete burning down of the coals or temperature will not be increased." The
  two differ by stage: p.92 sets the atmosphere by the interval; p.94 warns that letting the coals
  burn out entirely stops the climb.
- **Chamber firebox (himae)** (p.92, Read): a 9 in (23 cm) trough along the front wall, with a row
  of empty saggars at the bottom as the bagwall. Without a sunken firebox, leave a channel at least
  9 in wide between the setting and the front wall, the full height of the stacking (p.205).
- **Bundle schedule** (p.93, Read): the firebox is stoked first, 15 h to 1832 °F (1000 °C). Then
  each chamber's sides: ¼, ½ and ¾ bundle per stoking in hours 1–3; from hour 4 one bundle every
  3–4 minutes (reducing); up to two bundles per stoking in hour 7. About 75 bundles per side and
  7 h of stoking for each reduction chamber; about 5 h for oxidation chambers. A bundle is 15 in
  in diameter and 18 in long (the inches are washed out at the gutter; the metric beside them,
  "38cm in diameter by 18in./46cm long", is clear: 38 cm ÷ 2.54 = 14.96 in, agreeing with 15 in;
  Checked on the page image, 2026-10-30). One such seven-chamber kiln fired to cone 8–9 in about 56 h.
- **Building a climbing kiln** (p.94–95, Read): lean the front wall in up to the arch springing
  (38 in in his example); set the arch peak back from the chamber centre-line so its thrust goes
  to the common wall and on to the chimney; brace the first chamber's front wall while arching;
  wedge each arch from the springing to the top centre on both sides; build the chambers first,
  then the firebox; overcoat the kiln with 3 in of fireclay, dirt and straw.
- **Steps between chambers** (Read): 12–15 in (p.97); in the Bizen kiln a 5 in rise per 12 in, and
  none between the last chamber and the chimney (p.101).
- **Bizen design aims** (p.99, Read): a large chamber area for direct flashing; low chamber draft
  for the most ash deposit; varied chambers for varied atmosphere; the most efficient use of wood
  over a seven-day firing with minimum stoking and draft; minimum heat loss during a slow rise. Its
  walls (§3) cope with expansion by a long, slow firing and cooling (p.100).
- **Small flue into a small chamber, bigger flue out** (p.100, Read): 3 × 6 in inlets into a small
  chamber speed the draft through it; 6 × 9 in outlets avoid back pressure; the draft then slows in
  the large first chamber. The flue-stack opening into the chimney equals the height of the last
  chamber's exit flues, 10 in (p.101).
- **Stacking for draft** (p.102–103, Read): leave 3 × 5 in under-draft spaces across the floor
  beneath the shelves and about 6 in between the top setter and the roof; a tapering chamber back
  and small exit flues force the draft down through the ware.
- **Stoking signal** (p.104, Read): throw in small wood until a 12 in flame shows at the spy hole,
  let it burn down, repeat. Saggar cones at spy holes top, bottom and rear side tell the
  temperature, but fire colour usually decides.
- **Cooling** (p.105, Read): at final temperature in the last chamber, close all spy holes and
  dampers tight; a Bizen kiln cools very slowly, over about three days.
- **Charcoal** (p.106, Read): a bricked-up door pattern with a charcoal port at every shelf level
  lets charcoal be dropped on the ware at final temperature with a long push shovel; the chamber
  is then closed tight before moving to the next.
- Bizen bodies (no engine use): a refined 4–5 % iron clay with a grey Shigaraki-type stoneware, or
  60 % rice-paddy clay with 10 % or more iron, aged a year, with sea-sediment and yellow mountain
  clays; they bloat, blister, crack and sag if fired too fast (p.99, Read).

### Tunnel kilns

- **European tunnel kilns** (p.109–111, Read) were fired only from the firebox, not side-stoked, so
  the stacking didn't segment them. The La Borne kilns "ranged in size from 1766 to 2825 cu. ft
  (50 to 80 cu. m)". Ware went in through the back, and that entrance, bricked up, served as the
  exit flue; two later chimneys, one each side of it, "helps in the overall firing" (p.110).
  - Temperatures fall along the kiln: about 2372 °F (1300 °C) by the firebox, 1200 °C in the
    middle, about 2012 °F (1100 °C) at the rear, so the ware runs high-fire, middle-fire and
    earthenware by thirds (p.111).
  - Firing (p.111): 2½–3 days of preheating, up to four days of *petit feu* with split logs, then
    about one day of *grand feu* with bundles of branches and twigs for intense heat, a very long
    flame, good reduction and a hotter back; light salting of the firebox near the end.
  - Fireboxes, grates and bag walls: §14.

### Groundhog kilns

- **Proportions** (p.111–112, Fig. 4-85, Read): a rectangle with a full-width firebox at one end and
  a full-width chimney at the other; the chamber is like a cylinder sliced lengthwise at or a
  little off centre, the smaller half laid down. Average chamber 29–48 in high, 7–10 ft wide
  (drawing 5–10 ft) and 12–24 ft long (drawing: ware platform 10–20 ft). Firebox one third of the
  plan to two thirds ware platform with a shallow firebox, one quarter to three quarters with a
  dug-out firebox. A shallow firebox steps 12–15 in up to the ware floor; a dug-out firebox is
  42–48 in deep (text: about 48 in). Chimney inside 9–30 in wide; heights in §9. Kilns are dug
  into the ground (earth buttress, dirt cover) or built on the ground and buttressed with rock work
  or bracing. Shallow-firebox kilns are usually loaded from the front, dug-out ones from the
  chimney end, "more due to each potter's preference than … a rule".
- **Cool back corners** (p.113, Read): heat follows the easiest path, along the crown and the
  stacking. Obstructions such as columns that funnel the flow to the centre make the sides cooler.
  Cure it with equally spaced exit flues along the whole back wall and a centre damper above the
  chimney door to push the flow outward.
- **Length** (p.114, p.178, Read): side stoke holes make an extended length feasible and allow
  salting at the back. A groundhog fires evenly with good control unless it is too long and lacks
  side stoke holes; it is hard to reduce, because of the large air supply built into the firebox
  and no dampers in a large chimney (p.178).
- **Reducing a groundhog** (p.119, Read): cut the front air after temperature, open passive air
  dampers to slow the draft, perhaps add dampers at the chimney top; air only from under the grate.
- Grate-to-chimney ratio about 4:1 (p.77; §14).

### Multi-directional kilns (Ch. 7)

- **Principle** (p.178–179, Read): two cross-draft systems at right angles (an anagama-style tube and
  a modified groundhog) share one fire zone. Each firebox faces its own opposing chimney and is the
  chamber's door, so firebox and door size limit the size of a piece. The difference in pull between
  the two chimneys is the main control of draft and atmosphere.
- **Chimneys** (p.179, p.186, Read): split the groundhog chimney (and its collection box) into
  thirds, each flue with its own passive air and shelf damper, to draw left, centre, right or any
  combination; the single anagama chimney also has passive air and a shelf damper. Both are of
  equal height and slightly tapered.
- **Fireboxes** (p.179–180, Read): the design factors are overall size, grate-bar area, ash-pit
  depth and width, the step up to the chamber floor, air at the ash pit, the grate, the
  firebox-floor level and the stoke hole, and the entrance width and height. Give the smaller
  single chimney the larger firebox, the slow starting firebox with the most stacking; give the
  groundhog a smaller-entrance firebox leading straight into the chamber, for blasting. Fig. 7-3:
  firebox and grate "3–4' NORMAL" long; a 12–15 in step up to the ware platform; ash pit and walkway
  about 14 in (?) wide and 18 in deep (p.179).
- **Air at three levels** (p.180, Fig. 7-4, Read): below the grate, at the grate and at the stoke
  hole, each openable and closable, for blasting, choking and holding temperature and atmosphere;
  build the openings into the door when it is bricked up.
- **Fire zone** (p.183, Read): about 9 ft across for one kiln and 7 ft for another (Fig. 7-2 calls
  them diagonals), both about 3½–4½ ft to the vertex of the ceiling; the chamber height is optional
  and the fire zone can be shaped freely. A wide groundhog side with a steeper drop into the
  chimney flues lets shelves stack higher in the fire zone, good for blasting (p.187).
- **Fireboxes in practice** (p.188–189, Read): grate bars ¾ in apart; air holes in the door at grate
  level; the ash-pit entrance fully open; a 9 × 13 in stoke hole in each firebox door; a 9–12 in
  rise from the grate to the chamber floor, so smouldering coals fall into the front stacking.
  Don't over-toss wood onto the firebox stacking.
- **Two-firebox method** (p.189, Read): fire the anagama firebox with its chimney to high bisque,
  then build coals on the groundhog grate and blast to even out the fire zone. Bank the idle firebox
  with large logs on the grate and its air severely restricted. On each switch let the kiln cool a
  bit, then bring it back up; this builds ash. Blasting both at once is "fine once in a while", but
  the hot–cold rhythm makes the cross-directional effects. Either firebox can use either chimney.

## 14. Wood fireboxes and firing

### Grate against chimney

- **Grate area for natural draft**: wood 10 × the chimney's cross-section at its base (p.76); he
  now uses about 7:1 (p.77). The larger chimney gives a more forgiving firing, fires faster when
  needed, adjusts for altitude and leaves room to alter the flues, chimney height and damper
  (p.77, Read). **Groundhogs run about 4:1** (Fig. 3-10, p.77, Read). A side stoke's area counts as
  grate, and results round **up** to brick sizes (p.77, Read). When in doubt, make the grate and
  chimney larger (p.76).
- **Worked examples** (p.77, Figs 3-8, 3-9, Read): two 9 × 45 in fireboxes, 810 in² of grate, ÷ 10
  → an 81 in² (9 × 9 in) chimney; two 13½ × 45 in fireboxes, 1215 in², → "121½ sq. in. or 9 x
  13½in."; a cross-draft firebox 60 in × 36 in = 2160 in² → 216 in², 12 × 18 in, built 13½ × 18 in
  (243 in²); with a 9 × 60 in side stoke (540 in²), (2160 + 540) ÷ 10 = 270 in² → 13½ × 22½ in
  (304 in²).
- **Firebox floor** at least 10 × the chimney's cross-section (p.199, Checked); a fast-fire
  firebox's floor 10 × the chimney section plus 20 % (p.140). The fast-fire kiln's two 13½ × 36 in
  fireboxes, 972 in², are exactly 10 × its 81 in² chimney + 20 % (derived).

### Fireboxes

- **What a wood firebox must do** (p.199, Read): preheat the air; let it rise through the grate into
  a secondary combustion space; leave room above the grate; give easy access for raking the coals;
  and have properly sized and spaced flue holes into the chamber.
- **Where** (p.199, Read): a firebox outside the chamber preheats the air. One inside the chamber
  that is too small gives erratic temperatures and makes top temperature hard to reach. Bizen and
  anagama kilns set ware in the firebox (Bizen's is very large, and its long firing evens things
  out); a groundhog's firebox is only for firing.
- **Size by fuel** (p.194, Read): wood fireboxes are larger than coal, coal larger than oil; gas
  needs the least space. For a larger chamber kiln, enlarge the firebox in proportion (p.201).
- **Grate height** (p.199–200, Read): the grate at the vertical midpoint, half the space to the ash
  pit (Fig. 8-2, Checked); for outside fireboxes on 30–100 ft³ kilns, 12–18 in below the grate and
  the same above; the shortest, 12½ in with the grate at 6–7 in, for a 12–16 ft³ fast-fire kiln
  (p.199). On fast-fire kilns he puts more than half the firebox below the grate (p.140): their
  fireboxes are 12–15 in high on grate legs 7–8 in tall (p.204). A grate set too low chokes the
  coals; scale fireboxes up gradually as kilns grow, with ample ash-pit area (p.199). Chapter 7
  gives rises from the grate to the ware floor, which are a different measure: an 18 in ash pit and
  a 12–15 in step up (Fig. 7-3, p.179); a 9–12 in rise (p.188).
- **Equal volumes and ash** (p.166–168, Read): the Breda kiln's volume above and below its grate is
  equal, "a design factor that is critical" (p.167, Checked); clean the coals and ash out at the
  end of a firing or metal grate bars deform (p.168). A small firebox, about 3 ft to the floor dome
  with sloped sides, tends to choke; a cone chimney increased the draught, but ash build-up then
  choked the kiln; the standard European fix was a grate with an ash pit below (p.166–167). The ash
  pit preheats the combustion air ("free-breathing grate") (p.168).
- **Ash and grates** (p.194, Read): burn wood, coal or coke on fireclay or iron grates with enough
  space below for air and ash. Ash on the grate embeds the fuel and chokes the air. An ash pit too
  close to the grate also chokes the air and can "cause the iron grate bars to bend from excessive
  heat". The chimney keeps up the draft through the firebox.
- **Grate bars** (p.199–200, Fig. 8-3, Read): minimum, metal rods ¾–1 in in diameter 1 in apart,
  fireclay soaps 2–2½ in apart; maximum, fireclay soaps 4½–7 in apart; the metal-rod maximum is
  printed "??" on the figure, drawn about 3 × the 1 in gap marked above it, and the text says to
  set the largest gap by the size of the wood to be burned (the user's reading, 2026-10-29). All
  grate bars should be replaceable. Too wide lets
  big unburnt clinkers fall through and fills the ash pit fast; too close, clinkers can't fall, wood
  piles on them, the preheated air under the grate is choked and the temperature drops. Set the
  spacing by the wood used; maximum spacing needs a deeper ash pit.
  - Olsen's own grates are tighter than the 1 in minimum, on purpose, to keep the coals small and
    slow the ash-pit build-up: ¾ in iron rod at 1½ in centres, a ¾ in gap (p.204); bars ¾ in apart
    (p.117, p.188, p.206); ceramic tubes or kiln posts at 1 in (p.205). Treat 1 in as a guide, not a
    floor. Others: fireclay bars at 6 in (p.202); soaps on 9 in centres in a chamber firebox
    (p.205); bars 1 in apart, 37 of them running 8 ft into the firebox (p.167); widely spaced 3 in
    ceramic tubes (p.146); a ¾ in rod grate across the firebox (p.127); a 1 in iron rod built into
    the wall (p.131).
  - Coals built up to the bottom of the bars choke the air and "the kiln will literally stop";
    raking the ash pit reopens it (p.117). Keep the coals from touching the grate; with correct
    spacing only raking is needed and the coals never have to be removed (p.204).
- **Brick grates** (p.110, Read): a bonded brick arch with rows of nine holes, each "5 x 5 x 5 x
  7in.", or ring arches spaced 4½ in apart along the firebox, spanning the ash pit.
- **Grate to bag wall** (p.110, Read): leave a space about 2 ft (61 cm) wide between the back of the
  grate and the bag wall, for unobstructed preheated air and for raking coals into the ash pit;
  without it a clogged grate can choke the kiln. A bag wall of loose bricks laid in a checkerboard,
  the gaps acting as inlet flues, is always leant away from the flame.
- **Fast-fire grate** (p.204–205, Read): welded ¾ in iron rod at 1½ in centres; it bisects the
  firebox and sits as far back as possible, partly under the inlet flue. The flames roll two ways:
  into the chamber through the inlet flue, and back along the firebox roof ½ to ⅔ of the way down
  the grate. Cold air enters under the grate, is preheated by the coals and keeps the grate from
  melting. Pull the grates out after firing, or they oxidize and warp, then seal the fireboxes. A
  permanent alternative: a ledge at half height carrying ceramic tubes or kiln posts at 1 in
  spacing, reaching two-thirds of the way in (p.205).
- **Hob firebox** (p.203–204, Fig. 8-12, Read): no grate; a centre hob splits the firebox and wood
  is laid across the hobs at right angles to the draft; primary air comes from the top and pulls
  the flame down to the throat arch and inlet flue; the wood burns through into the ash pit, which
  runs half full of embers. Drawing: ash pits 18 in wide either side of a 4½ in hob, 9 in outer
  wall; clean-out holes 4½ × 4½ in; secondary-air holes 4½ × 4½ or 2½ × 9 in; a 2½ × 2½ in mouse
  hole. Start the fire in the ash pit; keep the hopper full.
- **Chamber fireboxes** (p.202, p.205–206, Read): a side-stoked firebox lets one person tend the
  firebox and stoke the first chamber from one spot; close its front at the end so the first
  chamber doesn't cool too fast (p.202). Climbing-kiln chamber fireboxes burn small wood, 1 × 1 to
  2 × 2 in and 18–24 in long, on grate bars at 9 in centres (p.205). A second-chamber firebox has a
  sunken ash pit under its inlet flue, the grate bisecting the flue (p.206).
- **Air at three levels** (p.200, Read): plenty at ash-pit level and at stoking level, plus
  grate-level air around the grate's perimeter through "mouse holes". A mouse hole is a covered
  channel about 2½ in square running 12–15 in into the ash pit: it feeds air to the coal bed, keeps
  the coal height down and adds heat (p.204).
- **Spreading the air** (p.116, p.206, Read): a 5 in extruded clay tube (square, p.206) with
  perforated sides runs under the grate, its holes smaller at the supply end and larger at the far
  end, regulated by a shutter or plug outside the kiln, so the air spreads evenly across the
  firebox.
- **A better chamber firebox** (p.202, Read): of one chamber-kiln firebox with two large air holes
  and wide-spaced fireclay bars, he would add more, smaller air holes spread evenly across the
  chamber, space the grate bars to the minimum, and add grate-level mouse holes that can be opened.
- **Air preheat**: every 400 °F of air preheat adds about 100 °F (55.6 °C) to the flame (p.199,
  Read). The page prints "400°F (22°C)"; a 400 F° interval is 222 C° (derived) (Flagged).

### Wood and stoking

- **Wood** (p.155–156, Read): the type of wood (hard or soft; branch, log or kindling) controls the
  temperature rise, the flame and the atmosphere. Hardwoods give short flames, intense local heat
  and good coals. Softwoods "explode quickly", giving a very long flame that moves heat quickly into
  the chamber. Logs 2–6 in in diameter give a relatively short flame and long coal life.
- **Rhythm** (p.160, Read): allow enough time between stokings for oxidation, set by how long the
  fuel takes to burn down and the atmosphere to clear.
- **When to stoke** (Read): when the flames at the chimney holes "suck back in" (p.168); when the
  flame sucks back into the kiln and the chimney has cleared (p.143); in a climbing chamber, when a
  12 in flame at the spy hole has burned down (p.104).
- Firing rules for particular kiln types: climbing and tube kilns §13; fast-fire kilns §12.

## 15. Coal

- **Grate**: 1 ft² of grate per 6–8 ft² of floor (p.76, p.206). More fireboxes allow a lower ratio:
  a kiln with four fireboxes runs 8:1, one with two runs 6:1; and "too large a ratio is better than
  too small", so err toward more grate (p.207, Read). Fig. 8-18's worked numbers don't reproduce
  the ratios: two 660 in² grates (4.6 ft², labelled 5) on 30 ft² of floor is 3.3:1, and four 4 ft²
  grates on 38 ft² is 2.4:1 (derived); the figure computes floor ÷ ratio (5 and 4+ ft²) and calls
  it the grate per firebox, though it is the total (p.207, Flagged). Coal and coke grate bars go
  closer together than wood grate bars (p.194).
- **Number of fireboxes** (p.206, Read): a rectangular chamber over 7 ft long needs extra fireboxes
  on each side, one per 6 ft being normal; a round kiln 7 ft across takes three or four evenly
  spaced; larger round kilns have fireboxes 6–8 ft apart round the perimeter.
- **Coal** (p.196, Read): destructively distilled bituminous ("gas coal") gives about 75 % coke and
  25 % coal gas, about 10,000 ft³ of gas per ton (283.17 m³ per 907 kg). Bituminous coal burns with
  a long flame and gains efficiency above red heat. Lignite is sufficient for low-temperature kilns
  and gives a long flame.
- **Stoking** (p.131, p.133, Read): above 2000 °F, a 250 ft³ two-firebox downdraft took 10–12
  shovels every 20 min for reduction and 3–4 shovels every 20 min for oxidation; its thick walls and
  only two fireboxes made the cycle slow (p.131). A round four-firebox downdraft of about 11 m³
  reached cone 10 in 19–20 h, stoking each firebox from 1 shovel per ½ h at the start to 5 shovels
  per 50 min at the end (Table 5-1); poorer coal stretches it to 30 h, and the top-to-bottom spread
  reaches 2½ cones, mainly by weather and coal quality (p.133).
- Coal, coke and oil need saggars against ash and sulphur (p.135; §12).

## 16. Oil firing

- **Fuel oils** (p.196, Read): #1 light; #2 diesel, a distillate, vaporized at normal temperature and
  pressure; #3 no longer refined; #4 a blend of #2, #5 and #6; #5 and #6 heavy black residual oils.
  Composition about carbon 86 %, hydrogen 13 %, sulphur 0–2 %. Fuel oil and kerosene burn cleaner
  than coal but are "dirty and inefficient up to red heat".
- **#2 diesel** (p.209, Read): 132,000–140,000 BTU/gal; specific gravity 0.85; 18,610 BTU/lb; 0.3 %
  sulphur. He prefers it to kerosene or #1 for its higher viscosity and "lower flashing point" (as
  printed). Kerosene and #1 also burn clean; used crankcase oil works too.
- **Combustion area**: 1 ft² per 5 ft² of floor (p.76, p.213); with forced air it "can be reduced
  somewhat", because the mixture is more intimate (p.213, Read).

### Drip oil

- **Plates** (p.207, Fig. 8-20, Read): three per flue channel, "the right number" in his experience;
  9 × 5 in, parallel, each 3 in below the one above and overlapping it 2 in; sloped about 2 in 9
  (2:9 to 2.5:9 is excellent for diesel; the slope depends on the oil's viscosity); a side lip, or
  flat plates if they are horizontal; iron, with welded-on pegs. Set them in loosely laid dense or
  insulating brick, with loose firebricks spanning the channels, so they can be removed and
  adjusted. The stack is about 16 in tall in a channel about 6 in (?) wide. Side-firing units:
  12 × 15 in iron plates spot-welded to an iron frame, the drip angle set by tilting.
- **Ports** (p.208, Read): at most one per 9 in (one brick) of inlet-flue wall, at least one per
  18 in. Starting 4½ in from the corner instead of 9 in fits one or two more. More burners fire
  faster.
- **Feed** (p.208, Read): by gravity from a tank 10 ft above the plates, with three gate valves on
  the main line; the oil drips into funnels and through ¼ in brass tubing onto each channel's top plate.
- **Firebox inside the chamber** (p.208, Read): like a side-stoking wood channel in a climbing-kiln
  chamber, at least 9 in from the kiln wall to the bagwall, running the chamber's length and up the
  wall.
- **Operation** (p.208–209, Read): light with kerosene-soaked rags at the foot of the bottom plate;
  keep the inlet flues and drip channels clear and break clinker with an iron rod; a little water
  poured down the funnels with the diesel breaks up carbon. Late in the firing the oil must burn on
  all three plates; if it burns only on the top one or two, increase the flow.
- **Fuel per firing** (p.208, Read): a 45–60 ft³ kiln with three drip ports uses about 60–75 US gal
  of #2 diesel to cone 10, about 130,000–220,000 BTU per ft³ of kiln per firing at 132,000 BTU/gal
  (derived).
- **A poor drip unit** (p.209, Read): two plates in a 6 in channel iron set straight into the flue
  channel. Carbon is hard to clean out through its open air end, the iron oxidizes away quickly, and
  its plate area, spacing and overlap are too small. It is good only for pre-firing until a
  forced-oil burner takes over.

### Forced oil

- **Water in the oil** (p.210, Read): start on diesel, then cheap or free crankcase oil; once the
  kiln is at red heat, add water through a needle valve, raised gradually to about 20 % of the fuel
  mix. Water breaks the oil up, which helps prevent clinker and volatilizes the fuel. A 9 in
  firebox with a curved deflector turns the long flame upward and greatly reduces slagging of the
  refractories.
- Denver-type low-pressure burners are inefficient at low temperature and efficient from red heat
  (p.210, Read).
- **Blowers** (p.211, Read): ⅓, ½ or ¾ hp motors at 2850–3600 rpm, impellers sized to the ft³/hr and
  pressure. Pressures run 4–48 oz, but 4–10 oz should be enough. Too much air, or air at too high a
  pressure, quenches the flame below its ignition point of 1200 °F (649 °C). For several oil
  burners, add up their air and manifold it from one blower.
- **Pump and nozzle** (p.211, Read): BTU/hr = GPH × 132,000 at 100 psi (p.214); a 5 GPH nozzle gives
  660,000 BTU/hr. The pump discharges at 100 psi, adjustable 80–130 psi, and flow goes as the square
  root of pressure: a 3 GPH nozzle gives 2.7 GPH at 80 psi and 3.43 GPH at 130 psi. A hot nozzle
  cuts the flow by about 6 %. So the 100 psi rating is nominal.
- **Ignition** (p.211–212, Read): transformer 120 V, 60 cycle, 10,000 V secondary; spark gap
  ⅛–5/32 in (3–4 mm); spark points ½ in above and ¼ in ahead of the orifice. The orifice sits ¾–1⅛ in
  back from the nose piece: ¾ in for 0.75–1 GPH, further back as the rate rises. The flame stands
  ½ in (low rates) to 1 in (high rates) off the nose piece. Aim for a cone, not a mushroom; the spray
  must not strike the nose piece (carbon); adjust the air until the flame tips are clean.
- **Turn-down** (p.212, p.214, Read): a single-orifice burner is always at full blast, so a slow
  bisque is hard. A three-stage nozzle adaptor (Table 8-4) solves it: #1 0.5 GPH (66,000 BTU/hr),
  #2 1.0 GPH (132,000), #3 2.0 GPH (264,000), 3.5 GPH and 462,000 BTU/hr in all; two such burners
  give 924,000 BTU/hr (271 kW). Fire #1 alone to 1000 °F (538 °C), #1 + #2 to 1850 °F (1016 °C)
  (printed "1000–1060°F"), then all three to the finish. The note lists six combinations though
  the text says seven are possible (#3 + #2 is missing).
- **Nozzles** (p.212–213, Read): 0.4 to 30 GPH, up to 100 GPH; H marks a hollow cone, S a solid cone.
  Table 8-1, by series, spray angle 30°–90°: R 0.4–3.50 GPH, solid; NS 0.5–2.00, hollow; AR
  0.6–3.50, solid; PLP 2.25–100, semi-solid; PL 2.25–50, hollow; HV 1.65–60, solid narrow (HV only
  at 30° and 45°).
- **Spray angle and firebox** (p.212–213, Read): a long, narrow firebox inside the chamber needs the
  smallest angle (45° or less); it is "perhaps the worst firebox design for oil, but fine for gas".
  A firebox outside the chamber takes a wide angle (45°+).
- **Where the firebox goes** (p.213, Read): outside the chamber, because of the flame's force and its
  slagging: the fast-fire firebox under the chamber, separate outside fireboxes, or a converted coal
  firebox using only the grate area, without the ash pit. Size the GPH from gas Rule 1.
- **Worked example** (p.214, Read): a 50 ft³ kiln on two opposed burners takes 50 × 10,000 BTU (IFB)
  or 16,000 (firebrick), divided by two. He doesn't finish it: 250,000 or 400,000 BTU/hr per burner,
  about 2.0 or 3.0 GPH (derived).

## 17. Salt kilns

- **Bricks** (p.15, p.126, p.136, Read): not high-silica brick (it slags with the soda), and never
  high-silica IFB; high- or medium-high-alumina brick is best; otherwise dense medium- to high-duty
  brick, at the cost of faster slagging (p.126). One 16 ft³ salt kiln used high-alumina insulating
  brick in 7½ in walls (or dense high-alumina with 8 in walls) and dense high-alumina (K28) burner
  port blocks (p.136). Mainly-stretcher walls let a slagged hot-face brick be replaced (p.42).
- **Chamber** (p.126, p.128, Read): a long, low chamber is excellent for salt glaze, because salt
  goes straight into the chamber above the pots (p.126). An overall chamber length of at least
  48 in (122 cm) helps even the firing (p.128).
- **Fuel options** (p.127, Fig. 4-153, Read): a 5 × 3 ft salt kiln drawn for gas, oil drip or wood,
  "with fuel combinations possible", takes 9 × 4½ in inlet flues for gas (two MR-750 burners per
  flue) and 13½ × 4½ in for oil drip or wood, with a ¾ in rod grate the full width of the firebox.
  Its width "can extend to 4 feet with added flues", with the wood firebox stoked from both sides.
  Its back usually fires cold, so a side burner or wood stoking evens it (p.128).
- **Salt** (p.127, Read): on average 1 lb (0.45 kg) per ft³ of stacking space for an excellent
  salting in a new kiln. A hard-brick salt kiln takes two to three firings with "enormous amounts of
  salt" to break in, and less as it matures. Use draw tiles.
- **Salting** (p.128, Read): start at maximum temperature, cone 9–10. Salt can go in on split bamboo
  halves about 2½ in in diameter, as long as the kiln is wide, flipped over above the pots (good
  for up to three firings). With four salt holes, salt the firebox and the first top hole, then
  5–10 min later the top and back holes; all four at once needs 15–20 min to recover. Each salting
  drops the temperature at least 101 °F (56 °C). Throw firebox salt over the bag wall, never drop
  it in. Reducing salt: close the dampers, salt, let the pressure build, then open; oxidizing salt:
  dampers open, fuel cut back a little. Never salt through burner ports or flues (they slag).
- **Salt ports** (p.137, Read): place them to salt straight into a burner's flame, for even
  salting.
- Fiber suffers from glaze volatiles (p.38; §5); nitride-bonded SiC shelves are usable in soda
  firing (p.35; §3).

## 18. Electric kilns

### Planning and construction

- **Principles** (p.244, Read): electric kilns use only design principles 1 and 9 (§6). His
  calculations are "not an installation guide": installation needs a licensed electrician.
- **Size and shape** (p.244–245, Read): the largest cube that heats its centre well is 30 × 30 ×
  30 in (p.244). The widest electric car kiln he knows is 45 in inside (45 × 106 × 49 in). Kilns of
  8 ft³ and up are usually 22–26 in wide, to take standard 20–24 in shelves; once the width is set,
  add length or height, and over 20 ft³ add length. Top-loaders are simplest to build but hard on
  the back; large electric kilns tend to be car kilns or have a door at each end. Styles: top-loader,
  front-loader, polygonal top-loader, sectional polygonal top-loader (p.245).
- **Bricks touching elements** (p.246, Read): Fe₂O₃ under 1 %, alkali 0.4 % or less, alumina at
  least 40–45 % (§3). A 2300 °F IFB can be used up to cone 9 or 2300 °F (1260 °C); cone 10–11 and
  above need 2600 °F IFB. (p.25–26 lets K-23 serve cold spots in cone 10 fuel kilns.) Coat every
  surface a Kanthal element touches with alumina or a high-quality refractory cement: the element
  lives by its oxide layer, which iron oxide, alkalis and glaze or enamel vapour attack.
- **Grooves** (p.246–247, Read): a square notch (parallel saw cuts to depth with the slivers broken
  out; the text prints "¼in. (6mm) apart" (?)) or a cylindrical groove (drilled along the 9 in
  length near a corner or the middle, then sawn open). Groove width 1.25 × the coil's outside
  diameter (p.247). Leave room for free radiation and for changing elements; don't recess the coil
  too deeply or narrow the opening.
- **Element blocks** (p.247, Read): commercial kilns hold elements on rods in ceramic blocks
  (Figs 9-7, 9-8; one block 9 in long and 2½ or 3 in high). The element radiates all round and lasts
  longer, and lower-grade or less pure IFB can be used because the element touches only the blocks.
- **Element spacing** (p.247, Read): rows usually one brick course, 2½ in, apart. Some smaller kilns
  put grooves as close as ¾ in within a brick, with 2½ in between groups (Fig. 9-11). Kanthal's
  support bricks: 3 in between elements for large furnaces, 2½ in for medium, about 1 in for small
  (Fig. 9-14). Change rows with a 45° groove in the transition brick (Fig. 9-12); turn floor
  elements with a hairpin (Fig. 9-13).
- **Walls** (p.248, Read): no taller than 48 in unless tied to the frame. Three 7 in walls (Fig. 9-16):
  4 in of high-temperature M-Board (grooved hot face), 2 in of low-temperature back-up board, a 1 in
  dead-man air space and a louvred metal shell; or a grooved IFB stretcher course backed by 2½ in of
  block insulation in a metal case; or a stretcher course backed by a K-20 rowlock course in a metal
  case. Kilns under 20 × 20 × 20 in usually have 4½ in walls with a stainless-steel backing.
  Polygonal kilns to cone 6 have 2½ in rowlock walls. One maker's 6 in wall is a single grooved
  M-Board unit, grooves 2½ in apart, with a louvred heat shield and a dead-man air space.
- **Vents** (p.248–249, Read): most electric kilns have vent holes high in a side wall or in the
  ceiling; any kiln over 8 ft³ needs a vent hole in the roof for moisture. A 22 × 52 in lid has one
  2½ × 2½ in vent in each half, on the centreline (p.249).
- **Polygonal kilns** (p.249, Read): 2½ in rowlock or 4½ in stretcher walls by maximum temperature;
  fiber back-up optional; stainless-steel banding. Lay them out on the 9 in brick: choose the
  diameter from the need and the shelf size; draw the inside and outside radii; set a 9 in chord
  touching the outside radius; run strings from the centre to the brick's outer corners and cut
  along them to make the template (Fig. 9-21). Groove every brick before banding (two grooves per
  rowlock brick, one per stretcher, Fig. 9-22); round off the outer corners; band with stainless
  sheet tightened by welded-on hose clamps (Fig. 9-23); no mortar. The lid is bonded stretchers cut
  to the polygon, mortared and banded (Fig. 9-24).
- **Reduction** (p.250, Read): smoke from an outside wood firebox, not heat, enters through floor
  inlet flues and leaves by an exit flue and chimney, both opened when reduction begins; stoke
  kindling, let the smoke clear a little, repeat. In the drawing the floor flue holes grow away from
  the firebox (¾, 1¼ and 1½ in). Reduction shortens element life: alternate reduction and oxidation
  firings, or bisque between reduction firings, to rebuild the oxide coat.
- Frames and flat roofs: §2.

### Element design

- **Starting** (p.257–258, Read): know the top temperature (for Ct, Tables 9-3 to 9-5), the voltage
  (from the service panel), the volume (so the kW) and the number of circuits; then assume the
  circuit count and the surface load (6.4–9 W/in² for cone 10). Use as much wire as possible, for
  even heat: split the total kW into smaller groups, each within the wire's acceptable surface
  load.
- **Wire size** (p.258, Read): in²/Ω = I² × Ct ÷ p, where I is the current of **one element group**,
  not the kiln's total. Choose the gauge whose in²/Ω (68 °F) meets the result.
- **Ohm's-law check** (p.258–259, Read): per group, Rh = E² ÷ P (hot); Rc = Rh ÷ Ct (cold); then
  in²/Ω = P ÷ (p × Rc) should land on the same gauge; element length L = Rc ÷ (Ω/ft at 68 °F).
- **Actual surface load** (p.259, Read): W/in² = watts per wire ÷ (diameter in × π × length ft ×
  12). Recompute in²/Ω with the actual load to confirm the gauge.
- **Coil** (p.259–260, Read): outside diameter D = 5–6 × the wire diameter d (Kanthal), stated for
  temperatures above 1830 °F (999 °C). Turns W = 12 L ÷ (π (D − d)), L in ft; close-wound length
  Lw = W × d; stretched to a pitch S of 2 × the wire, giving a length of (S ÷ d) × Lw.
- **Three checks at the end** (p.260, Read): is the surface load reasonable for the temperature?
  Does the wire size suit the temperature and W/in²? Will the stretched coil fit the space? If it
  won't fit, go to a thinner wire ("perhaps B&S 11 or 12"), which raises the W/in² and shortens the
  wire.
- **Coiling** (p.260, Read): makers wind on steel mandrels in a lathe, close-wound, then stretch to
  two or three times the diameter; larger gauges are heated electrically to 1292–1382 °F
  (700–750 °C) and stretched hot. Uneven stretching gives uneven heat.
- **Worked example** (p.258–260, Read), a validation case:
  - 10½ ft³, cone 10, 240 V, 108 A, 26 kW; six element groups on six switches, 18 A and 4333 W per
    group; p = 8 W/in² chosen from 6.4–9; Ct = 1.04, Kanthal A-1's value at 1200 °C (Table 9-3).
  - Quick method: printed "18 x 1.04 / 8" = 42.12 in²/Ω, which is 18² × 1.04 ÷ 8 (Flagged) → B&S 10.
  - Rh = 240² ÷ 4333 = 13.3 Ω; Rc = 13.3 ÷ 1.04 = 12.8 Ω; check 4333 ÷ (8 × 12.8) = 42.4, printed
    "4333 / 8 x 12.2 = 4333 / 102.4" (12.2 is a misprint for 12.8) → B&S 10.
  - B&S 10, d = 0.102 in, with 0.083 Ω/ft used: L = 12.8 ÷ 0.083 = 154.2 ft per group (about 925 ft
    for six, derived). Table 9-3 gives B&S 10 A-1 as 0.08398 Ω/ft and 45.74 in²/Ω, the thinnest
    gauge that meets 42.1–42.4 (B&S 11 offers only 32.30); with 0.08398 the length would be
    152.4 ft (derived).
  - Actual load printed as 7.4 W/in²; its denominator 582 should be about 593 (0.102 × π × 154.2 ×
    12), giving 7.3 W/in² (derived) (Flagged).
  - Coil: D = 5–6 × 0.102 = 0.51–0.612 in (33/64–39/64 in); 9/16 in (0.56) used. Turns: 154.2 × 12 ÷
    (3.14 × (0.56 − 0.102)) = 1850.4 ÷ 1.43 = 1293. Close-wound 1293 × 0.102 = 131.8 in (10.98 ft).
    Stretched at a pitch of 2d (printed "S (pitch) = 20") to 263.6 in, "or 22ft (approximate)".
- **Nomogram**, Fig. 9-29 (p.261, Read): Kanthal D and DS wire, B&S 1 to 12: kW and amp scales,
  lines for each gauge, a family of W/in² lines, and wire length per group and phase for 220 V
  (90–250 ft), 110 V (45–125 ft), 380 V (160–440 ft), 127 V (50–145 ft) and a fifth row read as
  240 V (?) (100–280 ft). Its two worked traces are unreadable in the scan.
- **Advice** (p.261, Read): the electric kiln is the hardest kiln to build, because of the power
  control. Have your element calculations checked by the wire maker or an electric-kiln maker; buy
  wire pre-coiled. A manufactured kiln is hard to beat on price and guarantee.
- **Element life and maintenance** (p.261–262, Read): most brick damage comes from stacking, tight
  shelves, firing too fast and blown-up ware (debris in the grooves); keep the grooves clean. Tighten
  the screw ties of loose lid bricks on a top-loader. Heat a sagging element red with a propane torch
  before pushing it back with pliers; replace or add element pins. Check the controller,
  thermocouple and element connections for corrosion every year. Elements last "up to 100 cycles"
  at cone 5/6 on the maker's schedules, fewer at cone 9/10. Replace them when a firing runs at least
  2 h longer than normal, the kiln can't reach temperature, or the elements blacken, stretch or fall
  out; replace all the sets together.
