No page of Olsen read for this note shows a door fixed to the car, nor how the car's deck seals
against the walls. The user's description of a door fixed to the car's front, sealed with sand or
fibre, is general knowledge, not a source (the user, 2026-10-30); the book's one car kiln hangs its
door separately. The engine's car is unchanged: its end wall rides on the car, from
`kiln-hardware.md` §6.3 [S35], a separate source; Kaneko's suspended door (Fig 6-64) is not
modelled yet. Either way the opening is the kiln's whole end, so a door in part of a wall is
refused on a car kiln.

So in climbing kilns the door is in the side wall, where the chamber's arch, running along the
draft, ends; the walls the arch springs from (front, back, between chambers) have no doors. The
engine allows a door in part of a side wall of chambers in a row under such arches, and keeps
refusing one in their front or back wall.

The fast-fire kiln's door is the whole front wall, bricked up loose each firing in rowlocks or
stretchers, with no frame of its own beyond the kiln's bolted angle (Figs 5-35, 5-36, 5-41); the
fireboxes run under the floor along the front and back walls, stoked from their ends, so the door
is clear of them (Fig 5-37). The Cappadocia variant (pp.143–145) wasn't re-checked for this note.

Olsen describes no catenary kiln (Soldner's is named only, `olsen-kilns.md`), so there is no
figure of a door in a catenary's end wall.

So the updrafts' doors are whole fronts or a hinged door in a rectangular angle-iron frame in the
front wall; none shows a lift-off door or a lintel.

## What the engine takes from them

- **Whole-wall doors stay** (Figs 5-18, 5-35, 5-41, 6-61 to 6-63): the engine's door today.
- **Two heads for a door in part of a wall.** Brick jambs and a semicircular arch over them,
  bricked up (Tamba, Figs 4-11, 4-14, 4-15; Kanayama, Figs 7-17, 7-18, 7-25) or closed by a swing
  door (Kecskemét, Fig 5-42); and a rectangular opening framed in angle iron that a hinged door
  closes (Fig 6-69). Neither figure
  shows a steel lintel carrying a wall over a door; the rectangular frame of Fig 6-69 sits in an
  end wall under a sprung arch, which puts no roof load on that wall (`door-headers.md` §D7).
- **A semicircular arch's sizes are not given.** No figure gives the ring's depth, the jambs'
  width or a form's size, and the arch checks in the engine (HarbisonWalker's, for roof arches with
  T/S 4–10 %) don't reach a door arch (`door-headers.md` §D3.7, §D8). So the arched head is laid
  out but not checked.
- **A steel lintel** is the method of `steel-design.md` §S5, not a built kiln's. Its end bearing on
  refractory brick stays unchecked (below).

## Bearing stress of a steel lintel's end on refractory brick

Searched 2026-10-30 (the repo's sources, `door-headers.md` §D1.3, §D5, §D8, and the makers' sheets
already read): **NOT FOUND**. The makers' sheets give cold crushing strength and, for one brick, a
hot-load deformation without its load; none gives an allowable or design bearing stress, and BIA's
allowable stresses are for building brick. A web search the same day ("allowable bearing stress
steel lintel on firebrick refractory masonry psi") returned only building-masonry practice: the
lintel's end stress must not exceed "the allowable unit stress for the type of masonry used" (a
lintel guide citing BIA and ANSI A41.1; https://www.scribd.com/document/861625864/Lintel-Design-Guide,
Excerpt), and 0.25 f′m from ACI 530 for CMU (https://eng-tips.com/threads/cmu-bearing-area.445058,
forum, secondary). Neither is for refractory brick, hot or cold: not used. The engine reports each end's reaction and the 4 in
least bearing length (TMS 402-16 §5.2.1.3, `door-headers.md` §D1.5) and marks the bearing stress
unchecked.
