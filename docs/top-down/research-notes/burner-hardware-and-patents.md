# Burner and manifold hardware for a lid-mounted, two-arc ("half-moon") propane kiln burner, plus patents

Scope: the burner and manifold hardware that could make a ring- or arc-shaped flame from a manifold in a kiln lid, and patents on lid, cover or roof burners and on peripheral ring manifolds. Whole top-fired kilns are covered by other researchers.

Source-quality notes for the report writer:

## 1. Ribbon burners (cast refractory block, many small ports, blower premix): makers, DIY methods, port and plenum sizing, curved versions

### Takeaway
Pine Ridge Burner is the one confirmed commercial maker of castable ribbon burners. Its burners have a steel shell, a 1/4" steel baffle, alloy-steel metering orifices and a 5"-deep cast block rated to 3100 °F, and they come in a 7"+ w.c. blower series and a 1–5" w.c. series. DIY practice (Emmerling/Wayne Coe, Frosty's NARB, many IForgeIron builds) puts roughly 1/8"–3/8" ports, cored with crayons, straws or bolts, through a 2–5" castable block over a baffled plenum. It is tuned by trial. Builders blame burn-back on mixture velocity dropping below flame speed, often from too many ports. No source documents a curved or arc-shaped ribbon burner. Industrial cast-iron ribbon burners (Selas) are straight, 2"–36" long, and take no more than 1-1/4" w.c. of premix.

**Published DIY method: the Emmerling design (2006 Hammer's Blow article, hosted by Wayne Coe)**
- As recounted on IForgeIron: a body of "3x3x3/16 x 10 long" square tube, a 2" pipe inlet "with a baffel welded at the pleneum entrance," and "3 rows of air holes cast in place with crayola crayons. **Port, plenum and pressure rules of thumb (forum folklore, each tied to a thread)**
- Ratio rule: "a 13 to 1 ratio of solid burner face to orifice area," i.e. one 5/16" port per 1 in² of burner face. "Too many holes and the structure of the block will be weakened." — [IForgeIron 61583](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61583-looking-for-ribbon-burner-recommendations-on-size-and-placement/)
- Example builds: 27 holes at 5/16" with a 2" inlet; 19 crayon ports in 3 rows in a 2" x 7" block; a 12" burner with 24 ports. 1/4" straws used as cores. — [IForgeIron 61583](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61583-looking-for-ribbon-burner-recommendations-on-size-and-placement/); [IForgeIron 66529 "Ribbon burner size"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/66529-ribbon-burner-size/)
- Burner length: leave a couple of inches clear at each end of the chamber (8" forge → 5" burner; 12" forge → 9" burner). — [IForgeIron 61583](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61583-looking-for-ribbon-burner-recommendations-on-size-and-placement/)
- Plenum: "if your blower output is in the 1" to 3" WC pressure range you are limited to a maximum plenum size of around 180 cubic inches" (one builder's estimate; the search summary did not say which of these IForgeIron threads it came from: 61583, 50761, 66529, 48781 or 11187). — [IForgeIron 50761](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/50761-questions-on-ribbon-burners/); [IForgeIron 11187](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/11187-ribbon-burners/)
- Frosty's naturally aspirated ribbon burner (NARB): "19 crayon-sized holes, which he says are slightly under 3/8" (9.5 mm) dia." A copycat 4x4x2.5 block with 17 ports at 1/4" gave flames "jumping off the block"; drilling the same 17 ports to 5/16" was "much better." Frosty: "The diameter of the outlet holes isn't too important, Grant Sarver used straws." — [IForgeIron 71342 "Some NARB experimentation"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/71342-some-narb-experimentation/); [IForgeIron 62061 "Calculating number of holes"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/62061-calculating-number-of-holes-for-ribbon-burner/); NARB build video: [YouTube "NARB - Naturally Asperated Ribbon Burner"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WtQ2aMV82uk)
- Port length data point (a different builder, "Buzzkill"): "ports are 3/16" in diameter and 1.5" long"; a later build had "about 185 functional ports, 1/8" in diameter and about 3" long." — [IForgeIron 62061](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/62061-calculating-number-of-holes-for-ribbon-burner/)
- Turndown: "ribbon burners generally have a low turn down ratio because of the plenum behind the burner block" (forum assertion, no number). — [IForgeIron 45919](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/45919-more-on-ribbon-burners/)
- Mixing in the plenum: a perforated distributor pipe along the plenum, or turning the inlet 90° "so the air fuel mix wasn't being driven straight into the ports," which let one builder drop the diffuser. A taller plenum with the inlet mounted horizontally at the top spreads the mix evenly. Port count is tuned by blocking holes "with a block of wood" until burn-back stops. — [IForgeIron 61583](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61583-looking-for-ribbon-burner-recommendations-on-size-and-placement/); [KnifeDogs "Ribbon burner build for new forge"](https://www.knifedogs.com/threads/ribbon-burner-build-for-new-forge.48423/)

### Inferences
- The Pine Ridge design reads as the standard way to hold the flame without flashback. Small metering bores (fast flow, faster than the flame can travel back) feed larger delivery ports (slower flow, which holds the flame at the face). For an arc burner this suggests separating "metering" from "delivery": steel or alloy metering tubes set in a cool baffle, with refractory delivery ports. Pine Ridge does not publish it in these terms.
- A castable ribbon block can be cast to any plan shape, so two semicircular arcs are geometrically possible. The difficulty is even mixture distribution along a long curved plenum. Forum fixes (distributor pipe, inlet turned 90°, taller plenum) were all developed on straight plenums of 3"–12". The arcs around a kiln lid would be several feet long. Feeding each arc at two or more points, or using a perforated distributor tube inside each arc plenum, is the inference from those fixes, not a documented practice.
- The 13:1 face-to-port rule and the 1/8"–3/8" port range come from forge burners at about 1–8" w.c. They are a starting point, not a design rule; the user would need to test-fire them.
- Selas's 1-1/4" w.c. maximum premix pressure and cast-iron body show that industrial ribbon burners are built to sit outside a hot chamber and fire in. They are not built to be embedded in a 1300 °C hot face.

### Gaps
- John Emmerling's actual instructions (port diameter, port depth, castable thickness, plenum volume) could not be read: the Wayne Coe site TLS failure.
- No published port count, port size, BTU rating or turndown ratio for any Pine Ridge model.
- No maker found called "Forge Burners Ltd"; not verified to exist.
- No source for a built arc or ring ribbon burner in a forge, kiln or furnace.
- No numeric turndown for castable ribbon burners. Only the forum assertion "low turndown" was found.

## 2. Ring, annular and line burners (cast-iron rings, jet burners, multi-port pipe, Maxon/Eclipse line burners), and any use of a ring burner inside a kiln

### Takeaway
Cast-iron ring and jet burners are naturally aspirated venturi devices. Their ports are drilled or cast into a cast-iron ring and fed at low to medium pressure. Their output depends on jet size and pressure: about 17–19 kBtu/h for low-pressure boiling rings, about 110 kBtu/h for a 20-tip jet burner. Their maker guidance (Hones) is about "head space" above the ring and secondary air. Industrial line burners (Eclipse AirHeat/AH-MA, Maxon NP-LE AIRFLO) are cast-iron or aluminium sections that bolt into straight, tee and cross shapes. Their upstream air limits are 450–600 °F and their downstream limit about 1000 °F. They are duct air-heater burners, not hot-chamber burners. No source documents a ring burner used inside a kiln.

### Cited Findings
- Charles A. Hones Inc. (maker of venturi pipe and ring burners), "Design Considerations for Venturi Burners" (2019 booklet). A standard venturi burner "can be supplied with a half length of pipe, ten feet, of the same size as the burner's gas connection" (for multiple burners, the manifold connection). Longer runs, more than two ells, or several appliances need a larger pipe and a second-stage regulator. A venturi supplies only part of the combustion air at household pressure, "the remainder must come from an ample secondary air supply. This is the principal reason why no Venturi burner will operate properly in a sealed-in combustion chamber." A pipe burner "set up for natural gas" run "on propane ... the gas orifice must be reduced." "A single Venturi pipe burner should have head space equal to approximately two times the diameter of the burner pipe." For multiple burners, add a fixed head space per extra pipe, and for "standard Venturi ring burners head space should equal approximately [a stated percentage] of the ring burner's outside diameter." The digits were lost in extraction. — [Charles A. Hones, Design Considerations for Venturi Burners (PDF)](https://charlesahones.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Basic-Venturi-Need-4-Page-8-2019.pdf)
- Low-pressure cast-iron boiling rings (BES, UK). Double ring: "with 0.8 mm jets as supplied, Propane & Butane: 17060 Btu, 5 kilowatts." Large single ring: "with 1.3 mm jet as supplied, Propane and Butane: 18 769 Btu, 5.5 kw." — [BES 8862 double ring](https://www.bes.co.uk/cast-iron-boiling-ring-double-burner-8862/); [BES 19537 large ring](https://www.bes.co.uk/cast-iron-large-boiling-ring-single-burner-19537)

### Inferences
- Every cast-iron ring or line burner found has its body in a cool airstream: under a pot, in a duct, or outside a furnace wall. The highest stated limit is about 1000 °F downstream air, for the Maxon line burner. None is rated for a body sitting at or near a 1220–1300 °C kiln hot face. A cast or drilled metal ring in the user's lid would have to sit outside or behind the refractory, with only refractory ports or nozzles reaching the hot face.
- Hones's statement that "no Venturi burner will operate properly in a sealed-in combustion chamber" matters for a lid manifold. A naturally aspirated arc of ports inside the closed kiln would depend on secondary air drawn through the kiln. A blower premix or nozzle-mix arrangement removes that dependence.
- Line-burner sections bolt into straight, tee and cross shapes only. An arc would need a custom casting or a polygon of short straight sections.

### Gaps
- No source found documenting a ring burner of any kind used inside a ceramic kiln.
- The Hones booklet's numbers (ring-burner head space as % of OD, flue area per BTU, the share of air a venturi supplies) were lost in PDF decoding. A clean copy or the Hones catalogue is needed.
- No official Maxon line-burner temperature datasheet was read; the 1000 °F/600 °F figures are from a resale listing.

## 3. Venturi into a perforated or drilled pipe (DIY kiln, raku and "pin" burners): hole size and drilling-ratio rules and their origin

### Takeaway
The one concrete, sourced drilling rule is from a burner vendor, Allied Kenco. The total drilled-hole area should be at least 80% of the pipe's internal cross-section, as in 32 holes of 1/8" in 3/4" black pipe. Start the first hole about 4" from the venturi and space holes ≤1/2" apart so the flame carries from hole to hole. Hones adds the head-space and secondary-air rules. No sourced article on Marc Ward's or anyone's "pin burners" was found.

### Cited Findings
- "You need to drill enough holes in the pipe so that the total area of the drilled holes is at least 80% of the cross sectional area of the internal diameter of the pipe you are using." "Suggested minimum # of 1/8" holes for 3/4" black pipe is 32." Start the first hole about 4" from the venturi; space holes "1/2" or less apart if you want the flame to carry from hole to hole." — [Allied Kenco, "Pipe Burner Info" (PDF)](https://www.alliedkenco.com/pdf/Build%20A%20Pipe%20Burner.pdf) (read via search excerpt; direct fetch failed with a certificate error)
- A natural-gas pipe burner converted to propane needs a smaller gas orifice. An oversized orifice "will pass more gas than the Venturi mixer can supply with air," and yellow candle-like tips show too little primary air. — [Charles A. Hones booklet](https://charlesahones.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Basic-Venturi-Need-4-Page-8-2019.pdf)
- Kiln burner placement folklore: set the burner back from the burner port by "half the diameter of the burner tip" (Ceramic Arts Daily forum). — [Ceramic Arts Daily, "Making Burners Do's and Don'ts"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/7867-making-burners-dos-and-donts/)
- Kiln conversion example: a 3½" floor burner hole with the top of a Ward MR-750 set ½"–¾" below the floor "provide[s] for the proper ratio of primary to secondary air." — [Ceramics Monthly, "Downdraft Kiln Conversion"](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/Downdraft-Kiln-Conversion)
- Ward Burner Systems: found only as the MR-750 venturi burner with pilot used in kiln conversions. A forum commenter says Ward burners "will pass safety inspections compared to home-made burners." — [Ceramic Arts Daily, MR750 stand thread](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/20401-need-to-build-a-stand-for-a-ward-systems-mr750-burner/)
- Venturi backburning discussions for kilns: [Ceramic Arts Daily "Venturi burners backburning"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/39130-venturi-burners-backburning/)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic check on the Allied Kenco example: Schedule 40 3/4" pipe has a nominal ID of 0.824" (standard pipe table, not from Allied), giving about 0.533 in² of bore. 80% of that is about 0.43 in². 32 holes of 1/8" (0.0123 in² each) give about 0.39 in², or about 74%. So "32" is approximate, or it assumes a smaller bore. Use the 80% area rule rather than the hole count.
- The 80% rule keeps total port area below the bore area, so the pipe stays slightly pressurised along its length. That is what lets a drilled pipe feed many holes from one end. A long semicircular drilled manifold would need the same or more care: feed from the middle or both ends.
- These rules are for open, cold-mounted burners (grills, kettles, raku). In a lid manifold, the drilled metal would sit next to or inside the hot face (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No sourced article on Marc Ward's "pin burner" designs was found. Their port rules remain unknown.
- No primary engineering source (e.g. the North American Combustion Handbook) for drilled-port sizing (port loading in Btu/h per in² of port, propane flashback/lift limits) was read. This is the main hole in the sourcing of the DIY rules.

## 4. Flat-flame and radiant-wall roof burners: could one central lid burner replace a ring manifold?

### Takeaway
Yes, in principle. Industrial flat-flame burners are made to hang in a furnace roof and spread a spinning disc of flame across the refractory, with little forward throw. The Fives North American 4832 is one: flame depth under 6", it cannot flash back, and its smallest size spans about 58,500–234,000 Btu/h. The catches for a pottery kiln are that it needs forced combustion air (≥12 osi, about 21" w.c., at high fire), it is meant for excess-air operation, and it is unstable at stoichiometric in a tight cold furnace until about 1400 °F. So reduction firing is unverified.

### Cited Findings
- "North American 4832 Flat Flame Burners convert the refractory expanse of a furnace wall or roof into a uniform heat radiating surface ... These burners produce disc-shaped, tangential flames that scrub adjacent refractory and have little forward velocity." They limit "hot spots caused by flame impingement on work as close as 12 inches away." — [Fives North American Bulletin 4832 (PDF)](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- Construction: "cast iron bodies and heat resistant cast iron mountings. All internals exposed to radiant heat from furnace are stainless steel. Tile material options include both 3000°F and 3200°F castable refractories. Tiles are available in either 9 or 13 inch lengths," held by alloy anchors; "Four hanger rods are furnished with every 4832 Roof Section Burner." — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- Operation: "sealed-in, nozzle mixing burners which can not flash back." "Burners are normally selected for 12 osi or more air pressure at high fire." "Flame depth (forward travel) is less than 6 inches for any size." "In tight, cold furnaces, 4832 Burners are not stable on stoichiometric air/gas ratio." Until the furnace reaches about 1400 °F: open doors, run lean (excess air), or use the 4833 (narrower air/gas limits, -3 to -6 sizes). "For fuel gas other than natural gas, specify gas composition." Standard preheated air up to 750 °F. The flame spin creates backpressure, so unused mounting holes must be plugged. — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- Capacity table (combustion air scfh; "for Btu/h HHV, multiply by 100") at 1/4/9/16 osi: 4832-2 585/1,170/1,740/2,340; 4832-3 885/1,770/2,640/3,540; 4832-4 1,450/2,900/4,330/5,800; 4832-5 2,370/4,740/7,050/9,500. Gas pressure for sizes -2 to -6 is "0" w.c. (atmospheric or negative), because of the open-ended gas tube and air spin. — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- 4831 Flat Flame applications: "Wire patenting furnaces: Roof-mounted burners provide more uniform heating than side-fired installations"; also cover annealers. A later catalogue lists 0.2–10.0 MMBtu/h HHV and combustion air to 1200 °F. — [Fives 4831 bulletin (Techrite)](https://www.techritecontrols.com.au/cms/uploads/Techrite-Controls-Fives-North-American-North-American-4831-Gas-Burner-1635.pdf); [Fives 4836 roof section burners](https://www.techritecontrols.com.au/cms/uploads/Techrite-Fives-North-American-north-American-4836-Gas-Burner-015101693948.pdf)
- Bloom Engineering HTR (High Thermal Release) burners "produce a flame shape that lies flat against a furnace's wall or roof." "Capacities from <70,000 to > 4.4 MMBtuh - HHV (12 kW to 1,300 kW)," [sic: 70,000 Btu/h is 20.5 kW, not 12; the slip is Bloom's (science review T4)] "Wall mounted or roof mounted versions available," square or round port blocks, fuels including propane, "high thermal turndown" (no number). — [Bloom Burners & Combustion Systems](https://www.bloomeng.com/burners-combustion-systems/)
- Flat-flame hot/cold air burner from another maker: [Combustech FFHC](https://combustech.net/ffhc2)
- Photograph: the 4832 bulletin shows the burner with its 3/4" observation port ("as shown in the above photograph"). — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)

### Inferences
- Unit conversion: 1 osi ≈ 1.73" w.c., so 12–16 osi is about 21–28" w.c. That is well above typical forge or ribbon-burner blowers (1–8" w.c.; see section 1). A flat-flame lid burner needs a real combustion-air blower.
- Natural-gas basis: the 4832-2 runs about 58,500 Btu/h HHV at 1 osi and about 234,000 Btu/h at 16 osi. The 4832-3 runs about 88,500–354,000. These overlap the input range quoted by a cone 10 kiln builder (about 320,000 Btu/h, in a sibling researcher's area; see [Ceramic Arts Daily community](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/3808-building-a-cone-10-outdoor-gassalt-kiln/)). Propane needs a factory check of the gas tube ("specify gas composition").
- A single central flat-flame burner gives a radiant disc centred on the lid, not a perimeter ring. Whether it heats the outer stacks evenly in a round kiln is unverified. The radial spin is the closest commercial match to "spreading the flame around the inside of the lid."
- The bulletin's emphasis on excess air and its stoichiometric instability in cold furnaces suggests reduction firing (fuel-rich) is outside its design envelope. It may be possible hot (above about 1400 °F), but no source confirms it.

### Gaps
- Flame diameters by size are in Instructions 4832-2, which was not found.
- No source on running flat-flame burners fuel-rich (reduction) or in pottery kilns.
- No Bloom HTR datasheet with materials or minimum size details was read.
- Zeeco, John Zink and Selas radiant-wall burners were not researched (time budget). They are mainly refinery-heater products.

## 5. Material limits in a kiln lid (cast iron, steel, 310, 330, Inconel), castable ribbon burners at cone 6–10, and flashback when the plenum gets hot

### Takeaway
No common burner metal survives at the cone 6–10 hot face (about 1220–1300 °C). Gray cast iron grows and oxidises above about 350–700 °C; ASTM A319 elevated-temperature gray iron goes to about 760 °C. 310 and 330 stainless are rated about 1150 °C continuous and 1035 °C intermittent in clean air. Inconel 601 reaches about 1205–1250 °C. A Japanese patent says refractory-steel burner tips in a kiln roof oxidise once the kiln exceeds about 1300 °C. So metal must stay behind refractory and be cooled by the incoming mixture, with ceramic or refractory ports at the hot face. Pine Ridge rates its castable block to 3100 °F (1704 °C), and Fives rates its flat-flame tiles to 3000/3200 °F. Flashback in ribbon burners, per forum consensus, happens when mixture velocity falls below flame speed or the block passes the mixture's ignition temperature. The fixes are more flow or fewer ports.

### Cited Findings
**Cast iron**
- Ordinary gray iron "hardly oxidizes or grows at temperatures below 350°C in air. Under 700°C, its oxidation is slight but some growth occurs ... Above 700°C, both oxidation and growth increase dramatically" (foundry blog; moderate reliability). — [steel-foundry.com, gray cast iron service properties](https://www.steel-foundry.com/blog/gray-cast-iron-performance-service-properties)
- "When grey cast iron is repeatedly heated at a high temperature in air, growth takes place principally due to oxidation" (1937 Kyoto University paper). — [Kyoto University repository 2433/280172](https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2433/280172)
- ASTM A319 covers gray iron for elevated-temperature, non-pressure parts up to about 1400 °F (760 °C), with some classes suited only to lower temperatures in long service. An older ASTM symposium notes that A278 then capped gray iron at 650 °F. — [ASTM A319 (copy)](https://softbank.iust.ac.ir/Railway/Codes/ASTM-standard/ASTM%202004%20Volume%2001.02%20Ferrous%20Castings_%20Ferroalloys/A%20319%20_%2071%20R01%20%20;QTMXOQ__.pdf); [ASTM STP 48314S](https://store.astm.org/stp48314s.html)
- Industrial burner practice keeps cast iron out of direct radiation. Fives: "All internals exposed to radiant heat from furnace are stainless steel." Eclipse: flame-exposed parts are "cast iron or #321 stainless steel" in a duct-burner context with ≤450 °F upstream air. — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf); [Eclipse Spec 140](https://www.milnor.com/tkb/HFW5.x/HFW%20Attachments/Vendor%20Data/Eclipse/Spec%20140%20Bulletin.pdf)

**Stainless and nickel alloys**
- Nickel Institute Designers' Handbook 9004: maximum service temperature in air, based on 10 mg/cm² oxidation in 1000 h. Type 310 is 1150 °C (2100 °F) continuous and 1035 °C (1900 °F) intermittent; Type 330 is listed at the same values (that row was garbled in extraction, so verify against the PDF). — [Nickel Institute 9004 (PDF)](https://nickelinstitute.org/media/1699/high_temperaturecharacteristicsofstainlesssteel_9004_.pdf)
- Supplier datasheet for 310/310S: 1050 °C (1922 °F) continuous and 1100 °C (2012 °F) peak in oxidising, low-sulphur conditions; 950 °C (1742 °F) with higher sulphur. — [steelpipesfactory 310/310S datasheet](https://www.steelpipesfactory.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Datasheet-For-Stainless-Steel-310-310S.pdf)
- Load-bearing 310 fasteners: a practical limit of about 800 °C (aggregator; low reliability). — [Ray-Chin 310 fasteners](https://www.ray-chin.com/news/NEWS/Industry-News/310-310S-Fasteners-Complete-Technical-Data-Tensile-Strength-Temperature-Limits-Torque-Machining-More.html)
- Inconel 601: oxidation resistance quoted up to 1250 °C (about 2280 °F) by distributor datasheets, or "to 2200 °F" (about 1205 °C) by another distributor. It is attributed to "a tightly adherent oxide layer which is resistant against spalling" under cyclic heating, helped by 1.0–1.7% Al and 21–25% Cr. — [High Performance Alloys, Inconel 601](https://www.hpalloys.com/Alloys/601); [UL Prospector, HPA 601 bar](https://metals.ulprospector.com/metals/en/datasheet/218624/high-performance-alloys--inc-inconel-601-bar-1)
- Shinagawa Refractories patent: in a top-fired kiln, refractory-steel burner tips "wear from oxidation when kiln temperature exceeds about 1300° C and secondary air exceeds about 350° C," turn red-hot and block with carbon. Water cooling causes heat loss and risks explosion if it leaks. The fix was a silicon nitride or zirconia air nozzle, with secondary air fed at the nozzle tip to limit thermal shock. — [US4240788A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4240788A/en)

**Castable ribbon burners at high temperature**
- Pine Ridge block: "maximum recommended use temperature of 3100 degrees Fahrenheit," "specially formulated" for repeated heat cycles; used in glass furnaces and glory holes. — [Pine Ridge Burner](https://www.pineridgeburner.com/)
- Fives flat-flame tiles: 3000 °F and 3200 °F castables. — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- Ribbon burner breakage thread (block cracking): [IForgeIron 57667 "Ribbon burner breakage"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/57667-ribbon-burner-breakage/)

**Flashback and hot plenums (forum consensus)**
- The mixture "ignites at the first point in the system where the temperature is high enough and the flow velocity is slower than the flame front." "The ... most common solution to overheating and back firing is to turn up the burner so there's enough fuel air mix flow to keep the block below the mix's flash point." Too many outlets lower the port velocity and cause burn-back; ash-clogged ports give uneven flow. With less cooling flow, forge heat "creep[s] back into the burner head towards the plenum"; pulling the burner back helps. Plenum ignition has been survived ("all that happened was the plenum got very hot"), but a plenum too hot to touch is a warning sign. — [IForgeIron 61062 "Ribbon burner backfire question"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61062-ribbon-burner-backfire-question/); [IForgeIron 66730 "Ribbon burner overheat"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/66730-ribbon-burner-overheat/); [IForgeIron 61313 "Ribbon forge back burning"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61313-ribbon-forge-back-burning/); [IForgeIron 67845 "Metering tube dimensions"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/67845-ribbon-burner-metering-tube-dimensions/)
- Nozzle-mix burners avoid the issue by design: the Fives 4832 is "sealed-in, nozzle mixing ... can not flash back." — [Fives 4832](https://webasset.fivesgroup.com/Energy_Combustion/Products_-_NA/Flat_Flame/4832_bul-North%20American%20Flat%20Flame%20Bulletin.pdf)
- Makers' statements: Pine Ridge's site makes no flashback claim. Selas states only a 1-1/4" w.c. maximum premix pressure. — [Pine Ridge Burner](https://www.pineridgeburner.com/); [Selas utility ribbon burner](https://www.selas.com/product/industrial-burners/ribbon-burners/ribbon-burner/erb-utility-burner/)

### Inferences
- A kiln is fired and cooled every cycle, so the Nickel Institute's intermittent figure (1035 °C for 310/330) applies, not the continuous one. Either way, both are below cone 6–10 hot-face temperatures. Inconel 601's 1205–1250 °C is at or below cone 6 and below cone 10.
- In a lid, the ribbon-burner geometry turns hostile. In a forge the block's hot face sees perhaps 1100–1300 °C, but the plenum sits outside the shell in room air. In a kiln lid the plenum and manifold sit directly over the hottest refractory, and hot gas rises against them. The thicker the refractory between hot face and plenum, the better. The forum fixes (keep flow up, avoid low fire, pull the burner back) all suggest a lid ribbon burner will be most prone to burn-back during the low-fire, low-flow phases of a cone 6–10 schedule, especially candling and reduction holds. This is an inference, not a documented kiln result.
- The forge-ribbon-burner "low turndown" claim and the pottery need for slow early heating conflict. A nozzle-mix lid design (gas and air kept separate until the port) removes plenum flashback as a failure mode. Fives says so explicitly for its flat-flame burner.
- US4240788 shows the industrial answer to metal tips in a 1300 °C+ roof: ceramic nozzles (Si₃N₄ or zirconia) with the metal parts kept back in the wind box.

### Gaps
- No source found for castable ribbon burners in service in cone 6–10 pottery kilns, or for their life in that service.
- No maker statement on flashback limits versus plenum temperature.
- The ignition temperature of propane-air mixtures and laminar flame speeds (for port-velocity sizing) were not sourced. They should come from the North American Combustion Handbook or NFPA/IGEM data.
- No Special Metals primary datasheet for Inconel 601, and no primary 330 datasheet, was read.
- No source on how hot a lid-top plenum gets with a given refractory thickness. That is a heat-loss calculation the engine could do.

## 6. Patents: lid, cover or roof burners firing down; ring or annular manifolds around a chamber perimeter; peripheral or tangential top burners

### Takeaway
No patent was found for a pottery kiln with a lid-mounted ring or arc manifold, or for "two half-moon" burner arcs. The closest prior art is in ladle preheaters: US4014532 (1977) has several burners spaced around the periphery of a refractory cover, firing tangentially. Metal-melting crucible lids follow: US10598437 (2020) has three burners in the lid around its axis, firing down. Top-fired tunnel and shuttle kilns: US4240788 (1980) has ceiling burners with ceramic nozzles; US3854865 (1974) discharges under the roof and drives the flow down through the ware. Annular gas manifolds: US2567013 (1951) and US3076496.

### Inferences
- The user's idea combines three features: a lid-mounted burner, a perimeter (ring or arc) layout, and a cast manifold. Each has prior art separately: lid burners in ladle and crucible covers, perimeter tangential burners in a cover (US4014532 claim 4), and annular manifolds (US2567013, US3076496). No combined kiln-lid arc-manifold patent turned up in these searches. That is a search result, not a freedom-to-operate opinion.
- US4014532 (tangential peripheral burners in a refractory cover) is the strongest single precedent for "spreading the flame around the inside perimeter from the lid." Its stated purpose, more even heating of a large vessel lining, matches the user's goal.
- The US7637739 note that downward roof firing heats the top of the setting faster than the bottom is a direct warning for a top-fired kiln. The flue and draft path (US3854865's floor-level flues pulling gas down through the ware) is what evens it out.

### Gaps
- Google Patents and Espacenet were searched only through a general web-search engine. No CPC-class search (F27B 17/00, F27D 99/00, F23D 14/10 ring burners, F23D 14/58 ribbon ports) was run. A class search with "lid"/"cover"/"Deckel" terms, including German and Japanese crucible-furnace art, may find closer matches.
- Grant years for US4718643, US6540957, US4432726, US4494927, US3076496, US7637739, US8087930 and US11168941 were not verified.
- No patent found using the phrases "top-fired kiln" or "lid burner" for pottery or ceramic periodic kilns.
