## 1. Buoyancy and draft: what happens when the heat source is at the top?

### Takeaway
Studio kiln literature puts the burners low and lets buoyancy carry the flame up. A downdraft kiln then turns the gas down through the ware to a floor-level flue, and practitioners and one study report it fires more evenly than an updraft kiln. The only proven precedent for firing downward from the roof is industrial: high-velocity (about 80–100 m/s) roof or lance burners in tunnel kilns. Those designs rely on jet momentum and deflectors, not buoyancy, to push the heat down and mix it. A lid-fired kiln would put its burners in the zone of highest positive pressure, at the top of the hot-gas column.

### Cited Findings
- Updraft kiln: burners at the base, flame rises through the ware to the top. Downdraft kiln: firebox at the front or sides, flame goes over the ware, back down through it, into an underfloor chamber, then to the chimney. Updraft kilns are described as less fuel-efficient than downdraft kilns, and crossdraft and downdraft designs as "the most complex and efficient" **(snippet)**. [Ceramic Arts Network, "An Introduction to Fuel-Burning Kilns"](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/daily/article/an-introduction-to-fuel-burning-kilns/)
- An Indonesian university study (English abstract only) reports that a downdraft kiln gives a more even temperature distribution in pottery firing than an updraft kiln, and is more efficient **(snippet)**. [UGM repository](https://etd.repository.ugm.ac.id/penelitian/detail/63047)
- A testing-focused source reported that potters with "the most even temperature from top to bottom and side to side" were firing traditional downdraft kilns **(snippet; the source among these results was not pinned down)**. [CAD forum search results, e.g. thread 30608](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/30608-how-do-i-even-out-the-kiln-firing/)
- Olsen's chimney rule, which the repo already records: chimney height = 3 × the downward pull + ⅓ × the horizontal pull. This is secondary, via the CAD forum, and it is how a downdraft kiln is sized to pull hot gas *down* against buoyancy. [Repo: docs/research/combustion-and-masonry.md §4.1, item S37](../../docs/research/combustion-and-masonry.md)
- Riedhammer tunnel-kiln patent US 4,368,036 (H. Kremheller, priority 8 July 1980, granted 11 Jan 1983). Nozzles and high-speed burners have jets "oriented vertically from the top to the bottom". Deflector stones, "in the manner of a curbstone" with a rounded throat, turn the downward jets into a transverse circulation. The goal is "transforming the jets ... into a uniform kiln atmosphere" and avoiding "local overheating". Typical embodiment: 60 m kiln, setting about 170 cm wide and 50 cm high, burners every 200 cm on each side (100 cm overall, staggered), nozzle 25 mm, jet speed 80–100 m/s **(fetched)**. A search snippet places these burners "in the region of the side walls". [Google Patents US4368036A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4368036A/en)
- IBS markets the LanceFire, a high-velocity lance burner for roof-fired ceramic tunnel kilns, and claims it gives "more uniform heating of the stack" (manufacturer claim; page returned 403, **snippet**). [IBS Brenner LanceFire](https://www.ibs-brenner.de/lance-burner-lancefire.php)
- Ceric roofing-tile tunnel kiln (Poland): about 10 m wide, 338 low-power burners in the high-fire zone for homogeneity, and high-speed burners in the roof for preheating **(snippet)**. A ZI-online tunnel-kiln article in the same results says very wide kilns run hotter at the centre than near the side walls, because gas flows more slowly near the walls (**snippet; I could not confirm which ZI article this came from**). [ZI-online, Ceric supersized tunnel kiln](https://www.zi-online.info/en/artikel/zi_2012-6_Ceric_Technologies_designs_a_supersized_tunnel_kiln_for_a_Polish_roofing-1430042.html)
- Stack effect, from building physics: warm, less dense gas inside an enclosure creates positive pressure at the top and negative pressure at the bottom, with a "neutral plane" between them where inside and outside pressures are equal **(snippet)**. [Designing Buildings Wiki, Stack effect](https://designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Stack_effect)
- Kiln pressure is only meaningful relative to the room the kiln stands in. In a tunnel kiln it runs from negative near the entrance to positive through the firing and cooling zones **(snippet)**. [Ceramic Industry, "Kiln Connection: Pressure Control in Tunnel Kilns"](https://www.ceramicindustry.com/articles/91948-kiln-connection--pressure-control-in-tunnel-kilns)
- Positive pressure means "kiln gases ... exit the kiln through any leaks or openings", and at a peep "gases hot enough to cause severe burns can flow out and those gases may be invisible!" (D. Finkelnburg, *Ceramics Monthly*, Dec 2019) **(fetched)**. [Ceramics Monthly, Techno File: Kiln Atmosphere](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/Techno-File-Kiln-Atmosphere)
- Practitioners say "pressurizing happens from the top down", and they set the damper so that flame shows at both the top and bottom spy holes **(snippet; group of CAD threads)**. [CAD thread 21224, "Getting reduction at top of gas kiln"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/21224-getting-reduction-at-top-of-gas-kiln/); [CAD thread 17553, "Downdraft reduction balance"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/17553-downdraft-reduction-balance-question/)
- Bag-wall lore, secondhand: Olsen ("Olson" in the post) recommends a bag wall at least 8 in. from the burners. A bag wall is typically 6–9 in. tall. "The simplest fix for a hot bottom is to raise the bag wall ... often just half a brick" **(snippet; group of CAD threads)**. [CAD thread 6085, "Kiln design question"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/6085-kiln-design-question/); [CAD thread 30608](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/30608-how-do-i-even-out-the-kiln-firing/)
- Forge builders warn that top burners "have the possibility to recycle exhaust fumes", which raises CO (opinion, **snippet**). [IForgeIron, "Side or top burner"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/25166-side-or-top-burner/)
- Commercial top-loading gas kilns keep the burners below. Rohde's TG top-loader has the burners "underneath pulling the air in from beneath" while the exhaust leaves from the top. The maker calls this "downdraft flame control" **(snippet)**. [Hot Clay, Rohde TG-80](https://hot-clay.com/products/rohde-top-loading-gas-kiln-tg-80). Olympic Torchbearer burners are welded to the stand, and the lid carries only a port with a brick cover **(snippet)**. [Clay-King, Olympic 2827G](https://www.clay-king.com/product/olympic-2827g-torchbearer-gas-kiln/). A kiln burner-safety patent puts a four-nozzle manifold on the *underside* of the kiln **(snippet)**. [US 4,613,300](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4613300)

### Inferences
- In a closed kiln, buoyancy puts the hottest, lightest gas against the crown. If the burners are also in the lid and the flue is high, nothing makes the heat travel downward except jet momentum and radiation. Hot-top, cold-bottom stratification is the expected result. This is my inference from stack-effect physics and from updraft and downdraft practice; I found no measured data for a lid-fired studio kiln.
- To make lid firing work, the literature suggests some combination of: (a) a floor-level exit flue with a tall chimney, so the gas has to travel down through the setting (a true downdraft); (b) high-momentum burners, which in the Riedhammer and IBS precedents are 80–100 m/s jets, far faster than studio venturi burners, aimed down gaps or lanes in the setting; and (c) deflectors or a floor shape that turns the jets into a circulation.
- With burners in the lid, the burner ports sit in the most positive-pressure part of the kiln. Natural-draft (venturi) burners rely on a slight negative pressure at the port to draw secondary air. At the top of the kiln they would instead see back pressure, flame and CO pushing out around the ports and the lid seal, and possible lift-off or rollout. Power (blower) burners are the more defensible choice. This is an inference; I found no source that tests it.

### Gaps
- No primary text from Olsen, Rhodes or Lou was accessible. I could not confirm what they say, if anything, about kilns fired from the top.
- No measured top-to-bottom temperature data for any studio kiln fired downward from the crown or lid.
- The jet velocity of typical studio venturi or power burners, for comparison with the 80–100 m/s industrial jets, was not found in this search.

## 2. Peripheral, tangential or swirl firing around the perimeter

### Takeaway
Swirl from tangential burners is standard in crucible furnaces and forges because it keeps the flame off the load and lengthens its residence time. Practitioners still report uneven heating of the load, and burned-through crucibles where the flame touches. Industrial roof burners that use swirl rotate adjacent burners in opposite directions to get uniform heat flux. That matters for a "two half-moon arcs" layout: whether the two arcs swirl the same way or meet head-on decides how the flow behaves.

### Cited Findings
- In crucible furnaces the burner enters tangentially at the side, so the flame swirls around the crucible. Aiming at the crucible "could cause uneven heating and possibly crack the crucible". In another case a builder whose flame touched a pipe crucible "burnt a hole in the crucible, right where the flame from the burner hits it" **(snippet; hobby-forum threads, quote-to-thread mapping not confirmed)**. [Hobby-Machinist, "Newbie question about crucible"](https://hobby-machinist.com/threads/newbie-question-about-crucible.5203); [HomeModelEngineMachinist, "Hot water heater furnace"](https://homemodelenginemachinist.com/threads/hot-water-heater-furnace.19489/page-2)
- A vendor explainer describes tangential firing that swirls hot gas around the crucible. It claims uniform heating stops hot spots that crack crucibles "in 20–30 heats". No independent source supports that number, so it is unverified **(snippet)**. [Firgelli, Gas crucible furnace mechanism](https://www.firgelliauto.com/blogs/mechanisms/gas-crucible-furnace)
- Forge swirl, from practitioners: even with swirl, thin edges and tips heat faster than thick sections, so the work has to be moved. Angling the burner so the flame strikes the floor builds a strong vortex, and the floor is preferred over the wall for impingement because it tolerates heat better. A burner pointing straight down from top-centre splits the flame into **two counter-rotating spirals**. A single-direction swirl keeps flame in the chamber longer and lets the liner re-radiate. Swirl is "often overemphasized" **(snippet; group of IForgeIron threads, per-quote mapping not confirmed)**. [IForgeIron, "On heat distribution, propane forge"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/24378-on-heat-distribution-propane-forge/); [IForgeIron, "Burner placement"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61646-burner-placement/); [IForgeIron, "Forges 101" p.19](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/47439-forges-101/page/19/)
- Tenova TR flat-flame roof burners: "adjacent burners rotate in opposite directions", which the maker says "ensures a highly uniform heat flux distribution over the charge" (manufacturer claim, **snippet**). [Tenova, TR Flat Flame Roof Burners](https://tenova.com/technologies/tr-flat-flame-roof-burners)
- Honeywell (Eclipse) WHG "Wall Hugger": the flame pattern "promotes even heating by radiation from the furnace walls and roof" (manufacturer claim, **snippet**; the page came back empty when fetched). [Honeywell WHG Wall Hugger](https://process.honeywell.com/us/en/products-backup/thermal-solutions/burners-and-heat-exchangers/direct-fired-furnace-burners/whg-wall-hugger)
- In large tunnel kilns the centre tends to run hotter than the wall regions, because gas flows more slowly near the walls **(snippet; see §1 attribution caveat)**. [ZI-online, Ceric tunnel kiln](https://www.zi-online.info/en/artikel/zi_2012-6_Ceric_Technologies_designs_a_supersized_tunnel_kiln_for_a_Polish_roofing-1430042.html)

### Inferences
- In a forge or crucible furnace, perimeter swirl heats the wall, and the wall then re-radiates to a single central load. A kiln setting is a stack of shelves that blocks lines of sight. Ware in the middle of the stack would get less re-radiation from the walls and less convective flow than ware at the edges, so swirl alone is unlikely to heat the centre of a dense setting evenly. This is an inference; I found no kiln data.
- If both half-moon arcs rotate the same way, they make one swirl, and a swirl has a low-velocity core, which could leave the centre and the floor underheated. If they start from a common point and run in opposite directions, the two streams meet head-on on the far side. That makes a stagnation zone and a likely hot spot, which is an analogy to the "two counter-rotating spirals" a straight-down forge burner produces. Either way, the flame is meant to hug the wall, so pots set near the wall in the top course are the most exposed to direct impingement. These are inferences.

### Gaps
- No CFD or measured study of tangential or swirl firing in a *kiln setting* of stacked shelves, as opposed to a single crucible or forge chamber.
- No source on spacing ware back from the wall to avoid peripheral flame impingement in kilns.

## 3. Radiation from a hot roof or lid compared with convection

### Takeaway
At kiln temperatures radiation dominates heat transfer, because it scales with T⁴. Industrial "flat-flame" and "radiant roof" burners are designed to heat the refractory roof so the roof radiates evenly to the load, without the flame touching it. A hot lid is therefore a useful radiant source for the top of the setting. Radiation travels in straight lines, though, so shelves shadow the lower courses. I found no percentage split between radiation and convection.

### Cited Findings
- A patent citing Trinks' *Industrial Furnaces*: "In the operating temperature range of industrial furnaces, thermal radiation dominates heat transfer between any two bodies not in intimate contact", because radiant flux scales with T⁴ while convection scales with the first power of the temperature difference **(snippet)**. [US 4,045,211, "Method for increasing radiant heat transfer from hot gases"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4045211)
- A Purdue dissertation on gas-fired radiant-tube furnaces concluded that radiation is the dominant mode of heat transfer from the tubes to the load **(snippet)**. [Purdue, "Analysis of energy transfer in industrial gas-fired radiant tube furnaces"](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI9420836)
- US DOE: furnaces and ovens "can experience significant radiation losses when operating at temperatures above 1,000°F" **(snippet)**. [US DOE tip sheet](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/manufacturing/tech_assistance/pdfs/38851.pdf)
- Fives North American flat-flame burners. The 4831 moves heat into the refractory by convection and the refractory radiates to the load, with "minimum forward velocity" giving "uniform radiant heating with no flame impingement". The 4832 has disc-shaped flames whose "flat flame characteristics ... limit the possibility of hot spots caused by flame impingement on work as close as 12 inches away" **(snippet; catalog page)**. [Fives North American Combustion Catalog, p. 530](https://online.flippingbook.com/view/139515/530/)
- Baukal and Platvoet (John Zink Hamworthy), AIChE *CEP* (2013): flat flames give "better radiant coverage" than round flames. A solid wall "is capable of more-efficient radiant heat transfer than flue gas alone", which is why flat-flame burners use the firebox wall to set the heating pattern **(snippet; PDF not readable by the fetch tool)**. [AIChE CEP 2013 burner article](https://www.aiche.org/sites/default/files/cep/20130835.pdf)
- A low-NOx flat-flame burner patent: widening the flame lowers heat flux per unit roof area and "will produce more uniform heating across the wall and roof of the furnace" **(snippet)**. [US 5,813,846, "Low NOx flat flame burner"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5813846)
- Varga, Tatič and Lazić (2009) studied flat-flame roof burners in pusher reheating furnaces as a way to raise radiant transfer from the refractory roof to the charge **(snippet)**. [Hrčak, Varga et al. 2009](https://hrcak.srce.hr/en/35478)

### Inferences
- A lid lined to run as a radiant roof, with flat or wall-hugging flames washing the hot face, is the best industrial analogue to the proposed design. It heats the top course well by radiation without the flame touching the ware. The top shelf shadows the courses below, and those depend on convection and on the gas being pulled down, which loops back to §1.
- The radiant-roof industry fires flat-flame burners *through* a roof of fixed refractory. I found no case of them in a lid that moves.

### Gaps
- No numerical split of radiation and convection (for example "X% radiation above 1000 °C") was found. The search notes that it depends on geometry, gas emissivity and atmosphere, so Trinks or a calculation for this kiln would be needed.
- No data on view factors from the lid to the lower shelves in a stacked kiln setting.

## 4. The lid: weight, expansion, insulation, burner-port hot spots, skin temperature, hinges and lifts

### Takeaway
Lids of electric and gas kilns already fail in known ways: the hot face expands against the cold face so the lid bows, banding is stressed, lids crack, and hinges get overloaded when brick replaces fiber. Burners and a cast manifold would add weight and local heat at each port. The ribbon-burner literature warns that a refractory burner head conducts heat poorly and can let the plenum side reach ignition temperature. No measured cone 10 lid-skin temperatures were found.

### Cited Findings
- A brick lid "weighs considerably more" than a fiber lid, and the existing hinge "may need an upgrade" or replacement from another brand. One reply about a heavy Skutt lid suggested a counterweight **(snippet; group of CAD threads)**. [CAD thread 19651, "Rebuilding kiln lid"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/19651-rebuilding-kiln-lid/); [CAD thread 6663, "Cracked kiln lid"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/6663-help-me-decide-what-to-do-about-a-cracked-kiln-lid/)
- The lid "creates a parabola/arc during the firing from the expansion of the hot face of the brick, against the cold face". Restraining it stresses the lid and its banding **(snippet; CAD threads)**. [CAD thread 3278, "Electric kiln lid failure"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/3278-electric-kiln-lid-failure/)
- One user's brick lid cracked and broke its band after a fiber blanket was added on top. Another poster doubted the blanket was the cause (disputed, **snippet**). [CAD thread 6663](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/6663-help-me-decide-what-to-do-about-a-cracked-kiln-lid/)
- Propping the lid is "bad for the lid, and bad for the top rim". A hydraulic lid lifter lifts evenly, without the torque of supporting the lid at one point **(snippet)**. [CAD thread 15811, "Propping kiln lid"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/15811-propping-kiln-lid/); [Warmglass, "Lid lifter"](https://warmglass.com/phpBB3/viewtopic.php?p=338007)
- Counterbalanced hinges are offered so heavy kiln lids lift easily, close at a controlled speed and stay open (vendor blog, **snippet**). [Weber Knapp, Motion control for ceramic studios](https://blog.weberknapp.com/motion-control-solutions-for-ceramic-studios)
- Rohde HE electric hood kilns lift the whole hood hydraulically on a linear guide rail (electric only, **snippet**). This is a precedent for lifting a heavy top section, not for gas. [Rohde HE 330](https://www.rohde.eu/en/arts-and-crafts/products/hood-kilns/he-330)
- Skin temperature, unverified. One archive text reports that a 3 in. soft-brick wall at about 2195°F gave a bare steel skin of about 1150°F. Adding 1 in. of fiber brought the steel to about 750°F and the outer surface to about 200°F, and the author added 2 in. of fiber to the lid. This is **snippet only; the host could not be reached to verify**, and it is below cone 10. [BC archive extracted text](https://bchdp-i2.arcabc.ca/_flysystem/repo-service/2025-06/113177-Extracted%20Text.txt)
- Fiber wrapped outside an intact metal jacket can push the fiber-to-metal interface hot enough to damage the jacket **(snippet)**. [CAD thread 477, "Insulating a converted kiln"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/477-insulating-a-converted-kiln/)
- Industrial practice for cone 10 is about 8 in. of fiber lining on walls and ceiling, with refractories derated about 150°F below their rated maximum **(snippet)**. [Ceramic Industry, "Controlled Firings: Kiln Design Matters"](https://www.ceramicindustry.com/articles/87858-pottery-production-practices-controlled-firings-kiln-design-matters/)
- Ribbon burners, which are refractory heads with many ports fed from a plenum and the closest analogue to a cast multi-port manifold. "Steel and cast iron have much better heat transfer than refractory", so with a refractory head "the air flow alone will not be enough to keep the plenum side of the burner below the ignition temperature." Secondary air entering through a gap between the head and the shell helps cool the head (practitioners, **snippet; group of threads**). [IForgeIron, "Ribbon burner overheat"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/66730-ribbon-burner-overheat/); [IForgeIron, "Ribbon burner backfire question"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61062-ribbon-burner-backfire-question/); [IForgeIron, "Homegrown chip bed forge"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/10679-homegrown-chip-bed-forge/)
- Burner depth in the port: if the burner sits too far back in the refractory, "the end of the burner will overheat and the fuel/air mix will combust inside the burner tube". One fix was moving the flare to about ½ in. into the refractory (practitioners, **snippet**). [IForgeIron, "Burner depth"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/60966-burner-depth/)
- Lids already carry elements in electric top-firing designs (Skutt Firebox 14), but that is electric heat, not gas **(snippet)**. [Warm Glass UK, Skutt Firebox 14](https://warm-glass.co.uk/products/warehouse-deal-skutt-firebox-14-glass-kiln)

### Inferences
- A burner-carrying lid has to resist bowing and cracking while holding a rigid steel or cast manifold whose thermal expansion differs from the refractory's. It also concentrates heat at every port. That points to a separately supported manifold, with flexible mounting at each burner, outside the hot face.
- Because the lid weighs more, a counterweight, gas spring or powered lift is likely required. Lifting the whole lid evenly, as the Rohde hood does, avoids the one-point torque that cracks lids.

### Gaps
- No source on purpose-made burner blocks or burner tiles set in a kiln *lid*, or on their weight.
- No measured outer-skin temperatures for a cone 10 gas-kiln crown or lid. Measure with a surface thermocouple or IR gun.
- No published lid weights for fiber, IFB or castable lids of a given size were found here. The repo's materials library may have densities for that calculation.

## 5. Gas supply to a moving lid, flame supervision, interlocks, purge, and the codes

### Takeaway
Standard LP-gas hose (UL 21) is rated only from −40°C to 60°C (140°F), so a hose must be kept away from a hot lid or replaced by a listed movable-appliance connector with a restraint. Ceramic kilns fall under NFPA 86 in a vendor's reading. NFPA 86 excludes only *listed* equipment of 150,000 Btu/h (44 kW) or less, so a homebuilt kiln of any size is not exempted by that clause. NFPA 86 flame-safeguard rules, as reported secondhand, require gas valves to close within 4 s of flame loss and supervision below 1400°F, with purge exemptions for multi-burner chambers. UL 795 is aimed at equipment above 400,000 Btu/h and appears not to cover process equipment. I found no published rule written specifically for burners on a lid that moves, or for lid-open interlocks.

### Cited Findings
**Hose and connectors**
- UL 21 (LP-Gas Hose) covers hose and hose assemblies up to 4 in. nominal ID for closed systems under NFPA 58, maximum 350 psig, for temperatures from **−40°C to 60°C**. It excludes flexible hose connectors for LP-gas (UL 569), low-pressure appliance connectors of 5 psig or less (ANSI Z21.24), and hose for portable outdoor appliances (ANSI Z21.54) **(snippet)**. [GlobalSpec, UL 21](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/14551783/ul-21)
- NFPA 58 hose: designed for 350 psig (2.4 MPag) working pressure with a 5:1 safety factor, and continuously marked "LP GAS, PROPANE, 350 PSI WORKING PRESSURE" plus the maker's name. After installation or modification, piping including hose must be proven leak-free at not less than normal operating pressure (cited as NFPA 58 §6.16.1.1) **(snippet)**. [South Carolina LLR, 2019 Reseller Installation Guidelines](https://llr.sc.gov/lp/forms/2019%20Reseller%20Installation%20Guidelines.pdf)
- ANSI Z21.69-2015 / CSA 6.16-2015 (R2020) covers gas connectors for appliances "mounted on casters or otherwise subject to movement", for natural gas, propane and LP-air. Vendor literature says §1.7.4 requires a restraining device so strain does not reach the connector, and vendor data sheets list ½ psi maximum and indoor use. I have **not** verified that against the standard's text **(snippet)**. [ANSI webstore, Z21.69-2015/CSA 6.16-2015](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/csa/ansiz21692015csa16); [SCC listing](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/4028317)
- Near top-mounted forge burners, practitioners prefer copper line to rubber hose because "copper won't burn like rubber hose" (opinion, **snippet**). [IForgeIron, "Cooling forge chimney effect"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/69072-cooling-forge-chimney-effect/)
- One raku burner kit ships a 3 m hose armoured with an overbraid and tells users to leak-test all joints with detector liquid **(snippet)**. [Hot Clay, SEGAS raku burner kit](https://www.hot-clay.com/products/segas-raku-burner-kit)
- Laguna's raku manual: almost all raku kilns are installed outdoors with no combustible vegetation nearby, and tank and line size should be confirmed with the local supplier **(snippet)**. [Laguna Raku Kiln Manual](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0664/2050/6866/files/Laguna_Raku_Kiln_Manual.pdf)
- For propane construction heaters (not kilns), the guidance is at least 6 ft from any cylinder and a protected hose run **(snippet)**. [PERC, Construction Job Site Safety](https://cloudinary.propane.com/images/v1644945234/website-media/22PERC0061_Construction-Job-Site-Safety_HR/22PERC0061_Construction-Job-Site-Safety_HR.pdf?_i=AA)

**NFPA 86 scope**
- NFPA 86 (2023, active) applies to "Class A, Class B, Class C, and Class D ovens, dryers, and furnaces; oxidizer systems; and any other heated systems ... used for processing of materials". It excludes solid-fuel firing systems and "listed equipment with a heating system(s) that supplies a total input not exceeding 150,000 Btu/hr (44 kW)" **(snippet)**. [GlobalSpec, NFPA 86](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/14562808/86)
- North American Manufacturing (Lukacs, presentation to ACerS, 2007 edition of NFPA 86): "It is our opinion that ceramic kilns fall within the scope of NFPA 86 as supported by ... Annex A.1.1.2." This is a vendor interpretation, and the PDF itself was image-based and unreadable to me **(snippet)**. [ceramics.org, "Kilns in Compliance" (Lukacs)](https://ceramics.org/wp-content/uploads/2009/07/kilnsincompliance_lukacs.pdf)
- Industrial ceramic kilns "can be Class A, B or C systems" **(snippet)**. [Ceramic Industry, "What You Need to Know About NFPA 86-2015"](https://ceramicindustry.com/articles/95804-what-you-need-to-know-about-nfpa-86-2015)
- Some local fire codes adopt it directly. Seattle: "industrial ovens and furnaces shall comply with the applicable provisions of NFPA 86, the International Fuel Gas Code, International Mechanical Code and this chapter" **(snippet)**. [Seattle Fire Code 2012, Ch. 30](https://seattle.gov/documents/departments/sdci/codes/seattlefirecode/2012seattlefirechapter30.pdf)

**Flame supervision, safety shutoff valves and purge (NFPA 86, secondhand)**
- Flame failure: the safeguard must de-energize the gas valves within **4 seconds** of flame loss. Flame supervision is not required at or above **1400°F**, but it is required during warm-up below that and whenever the temperature falls below it. The 1400°F sensor should be dedicated, locked and fail downscale (Process Heating, May 2020, **snippet**; page body was empty when fetched). [Process Heating digital edition, May 2020](https://digital.bnpmedia.com/article/Combustion+Control/3659189/658227/article.html)
- Multi-burner repeat purge (NFPA 86 revision review, Intertek). A repeat pre-ignition purge may be skipped if the chamber is proven above 1400°F. Without that proof, it may be skipped if each burner has two safety shutoff valves that close for non-operating burners and at least one burner stays lit in the common chamber. A single valve per burner is allowed only for natural gas, butane or **propane**, and only with a demonstrated combustible concentration of 25% of the LFL or less in the chamber and recirculation passages **(snippet; PDF not readable by the fetch tool)**. [Intertek, NFPA 86 revision summary](https://cdn.intertek.com/www-intertek-com/dms-legacy/NFPA-86-Rev-5-24-2018-ED-5-24-2020.pdf)
- Vendor checklist: pre-purge typically ≥4 system volumes, double-block-and-bleed valving, post-purge ≥15 s (Emerson white paper, **snippet**). [Emerson, "What now? More standards for safety compliance"](https://d1-live.emerson.com/documents/automation/white-paper-what-now-more-standards-for-safety-compliance-deltav-en-56732.pdf)

**UL 795 and CSA**
- UL 795 applies to "factory-built gas appliances having inputs of more than 400,000 Btu per hour, per individual combustion chamber". An older edition says it does not apply to process equipment. The 2024 (9th) edition is retitled *Commercial-Industrial Gas-Fired Package Boilers*, with furnaces and air heaters above 400,000 Btu/h covered in the US only **(snippet)**. [ANSI webstore, UL 795:2024](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/ul/ul795ed2024); [SCC, ANSI/CAN/UL 795:2024](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/4032176); [GlobalSpec, UL 795](https://standards.globalspec.com/std/14800158/ul-795)
- CSA B149.3:25, the Canadian code for field approval of fuel-related components on appliances and equipment: Saskatchewan's interpretations document covers pilot and main safety shutoff valves and takes effect **1 April 2026** **(snippet)**. [TSASK, CSA B149.3-25 Interpretations](https://api.tsask.ca/wp-content/uploads/TSASK-CSA-B149.3-25-Interpretations-final-draft.pdf)

**Flame detectors**
- Thermocouples, thermopiles and flame rods are the usual detectors in pilot ignition systems. A "proved pilot" is one whose presence is sensed before gas is admitted to the main burner **(snippet)**. [BCcampus Open Textbook, Describe flame detectors](https://opentextbc.ca/plumbing3e/chapter/describe-flame-detectors)
- In thermocouple-and-pilot systems, the thermocouple current holds the safety valve open, and after flame loss "the hot junction cools within a minute or so". That is much slower than the 4 s quoted for NFPA 86 safeguards. One patent also notes the main burner "sometimes fails to ignite even though the pilot flame is lighted" (**snippet; the quote was in a group of burner-safety patents, and I did not confirm which one**). [US 5,718,256, "Burner controller assembly"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5718256)
- UV scanners: the scanner body is limited to about 200°F ambient, so it needs an insulating coupling or cooling air, and the sight pipe should not extend more than halfway through the refractory (Fireye literature, **snippet**). Hot refractory can fool visible-light sensors, and shimmer over hot refractory can fool IR sensors. A distributor claims UV is insensitive to hot refractory (marketing, **snippet**). [Fireye SC-107](https://www.fireye.com/Documents/SC-107.pdf)
- Studio kilns in practice: one older Bailey kiln reportedly used a Fireye UV sensor driving a solenoid. Geil kilns are discussed with ring pilots and Baso (thermocouple) valves (anecdotal, **snippet**). [CAD thread 3706, Fireye on old Bailey](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/3706-looking-for-information-on-fireye-for-old-bailey-kiln/); [CAD thread 37310, Geil 16F](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/37310-natural-gas-programmable-geil16f-shutting-off-at-1900-degrees/)

### Inferences
- A hose rated to 60°C must not run across, touch or hang above a lid skin that may reach hundreds of °F. The single unverified skin figure in §4 is 200°F outside the fiber and 750–1150°F on bare steel. The defensible options are a fixed rigid line to a hinge-axis swivel or a listed movable-appliance connector routed below and away from the hot skin, with a restraint, and a manual shutoff within reach. Z21.69 connectors are typically for low-pressure (≤ ½ psi) service, so on propane the regulator would have to sit upstream of the moving joint. These are inferences.
- With burners on the lid, opening the lid moves the burners with it. A lid-position switch wired into the flame-safeguard interlock chain, so gas closes before the lid lifts, is the obvious requirement. I found no published rule for it (see Gaps). After any lid-open event the chamber would need a fresh purge before relighting, unless the NFPA 86 >1400°F proof applies.
- UV scanners or flame rods mounted in a lid would sit in the hottest part of the exterior, so the 200°F scanner limit makes cooling or remote sight tubes likely necessary.

### Gaps
- NFPA 54, 58 and 86, UL 795 and CSA B149 texts are paywalled. Every clause number and value above is secondhand and must be checked against the edition the local AHJ has adopted.
- I found **no rule or standard written for burners on a moving lid, door or hood**, and no NFPA 86 clause on lid or door-open interlocks for fuel-fired kilns.
- I found no NFPA 86 premix-system requirements (flashback arrestors, fire checks), which would matter if the cast manifold carries premixed gas and air. The text is paywalled.
- No UL 569 requirements (flexible hose connectors for LP-gas) were found. I found no published maximum hose temperature for ANSI Z21.69 connectors or flexible metal connectors.
- No temperature limits for flame rods were found.

## 6. Reduction firing and atmosphere

### Takeaway
Reduction in a gas kiln is controlled by the damper and the chamber pressure, and practitioners say pressure builds from the top down. Ware nearest the burner ports gets the least reduction because secondary air enters there. With burners in the lid, both effects would be at the top, so the top course would likely see the least reduction and the most turbulence. I found no source that tests reduction in a kiln fired from the top.

### Cited Findings
- Pressure "happens from the top down". Setting the damper so the lowest part of the kiln is slightly pressurized helps reduction reach the bottom, and showing flame at both spy holes keeps reduction spread through the kiln (practitioners, **snippet**). [CAD thread 21224](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/21224-getting-reduction-at-top-of-gas-kiln/); [CAD thread 17553](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/17553-downdraft-reduction-balance-question/)
- In a kiln fired from the floor, "the pieces on the bottom shelves would be slightly less reduced due to proximity to the burner ports". In another kiln, horizontal burners hit the back wall and deflected upward, and most of the heat went back to the flue after the first shelf (practitioners, **snippet**). [CAD thread 28621, "Uneven firing in small gas kiln"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/28621-uneven-firing-in-small-gas-kiln/); [CAD thread 1094, "Uneven firing"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/1094-uneven-firingi-need-help/)
- Secondary air enters through the burner ports, driven by the kiln's natural draft, so the flame at the burner can be reducing while the kiln atmosphere is neutral or oxidizing. Closing the damper cuts that secondary air (practitioner, **snippet**). [CAD thread 6282, "Reduction"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/6282-reduction-s/); [CAD thread 1270, "Forced-air burners reduction"](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/1270-forced-air-burners-reduction/)
- A pressure-balance source warns that over-closing the damper can drop the neutral pressure point below the burner ports, so "flue products laden with carbon monoxide will vent from the burner ports", and that a flue outlet set too low relative to the burner ports makes this worse (**snippet; I could not pin down which page in the results said this**). [CAD/Digitalfire results set, e.g. Digitalfire burner book](https://digitalfire.com/burnerbook/index.php)
- "Full reduction effects in glazes can only be obtained before the glaze melts and glosses over." Continuous reduction makes CO, so ventilation is essential (Finkelnburg, CM Dec 2019, **fetched**). [Ceramics Monthly, Techno File: Kiln Atmosphere](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/Techno-File-Kiln-Atmosphere)
- A heavily sooted, high-carbon atmosphere "will decrease your reduction and waste fuel" (practitioner, **snippet**). [CAD forum results, thread 1938](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/1938-glaze-firing-in-gas-kiln/)

### Inferences
- In a lid-fired kiln the burner ports sit in the positive-pressure zone. During reduction, when the damper is closed further, the neutral plane rises, but the top is already at positive pressure. CO-rich gas would then leave through every gap at the lid seal and around the burner ports. Spillage at the lid seal and burner ports is a combustion-safety problem as well as a reduction-control problem (see §7). This is an inference.
- If the flame hugs the wall, reduction and any carbon deposition are likely to be heaviest on the outer and top ware, and lightest in the core of the setting. This is an inference; no data was found.

### Gaps
- No study or forum report of reduction uniformity, carbon trapping or carbon deposition in a kiln fired downward from the top.

## 7. Documented failures, accidents and forum warnings

### Takeaway
The closest analogues give a consistent warning list. Top-mounted forge burners act as chimneys after shutdown and overheat. Ribbon-burner plenums (a multi-port head on a common plenum) flash back when turned down or hot. Brass fittings, burner tubes and rubber hoses near top burners overheat or burn. Regulators frost on heavy draw. Kiln-related CO and gas-explosion incidents trace to missing monitoring, poor ventilation and bad burner operation. I found no documented accident with a gas kiln that has lid burners, because I found no such kiln.

### Cited Findings
- Chimney effect: "Because your burners are top mounted there will always be heat rising up through the mixing tube when you shut down the burners", and the chamber "will heat the burner like a chimney". Mitigations people use are foil over the air ports, angling the burner, removing it after use, and copper rather than rubber line (practitioners, **snippet; IForgeIron thread group**). [IForgeIron, "Cooling forge chimney effect"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/69072-cooling-forge-chimney-effect/); [IForgeIron, "Side or top burner"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/25166-side-or-top-burner/)
- On restart after heat soak, the end of the mixing tube nearest the chamber can get hot enough for ignition to happen *inside* the tube. Flames "should always be at the forge end of the mixing tube and never inside the tube itself" (practitioners, **snippet**). [IForgeIron, "Question about temperature build-up on an atmospheric burner"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/32597-question-about-temperature-build-up-on-an-atmospheric-burner/); [IForgeIron, "T-burner getting very hot"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/60744-t-burner-getting-very-hot/)
- Commercial-product report: a Vevor two-burner gas forge with "tubes glowing and fire in the choke holes after couple hours usage" (thread title). Another thread is titled "forge T fitting and brass fittings getting hot on burner" **(snippet)**. [IForgeIron thread 77375](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/77375-vevor-2-burner-gas-forge-tubes-glowing-and-fire-in-the-choke-holes-after-couple-hours-usage/); [IForgeIron thread 65618](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/65618-forge-t-fitting-and-brass-fittings-getting-hot-on-burner/)
- Ribbon-burner (multi-port plenum) flashback. At high flow the mixture carries heat out through the ports. Turned down, heat "creeps back into the burner head towards the plenum", and "the mixture in the plenum can ignite. This is usually not dangerous but will trash the burner quite quickly." Burnback occurs when "your flame front exceeds the velocity of the gas exiting the hole." Suggested fixes: fewer ports (one test is plugging about a third with Kaowool), not oversizing the burner, higher pressure, and high airflow with a richer, cooler mix (practitioners, **snippet; thread group**). [IForgeIron, "Ribbon burner overheat"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/66730-ribbon-burner-overheat/); [IForgeIron, "Ribbon burner forge issues"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/64902-ribbon-burner-forge-issues/); [IForgeIron, "Ribbon burner backfire question"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/61062-ribbon-burner-backfire-question/)
- For fast emergency shutoff, practitioners fit a quarter-turn ball valve on the main line, because a tank valve takes several seconds to close (opinion, **snippet**). [IForgeIron thread group, e.g. "Cooling forge chimney effect"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/69072-cooling-forge-chimney-effect/)
- Regulator frost: as propane expands through the regulator it drops to sub-zero temperatures and frosts. This is usually harmless, but liquid propane entering the regulator causes severe icing and can stop it working **(snippet)**. [Propane101, "Freezing propane regulators"](https://mail.propane101.com/propaneregulatorfreezing.htm). Heavy draw from small tanks makes them frost **(snippet)**. [IForgeIron, "Regulator freezing"](https://www.iforgeiron.com/topic/69757-regulator-freezing/)
- Hose fire: a user reported a regulator hose burning through with flame coming from the hose, and said the tank's excess-flow device did not stop the flow fast enough, so he closed the valve by hand (anecdote, **snippet; the results did not say which forum the account came from**). [Smoking Meat Forums results, e.g. "Propane regulator heater"](https://www.smokingmeatforums.com/threads/propane-regulator-heater.105018/post-614489)
- CO from a gas-fired kiln. Hixon, BC sawmill dry kiln, March 2023: three people were taken to hospital and one collapsed. Without sufficient ventilation, CO entered occupied spaces through unsealed openings, and "there were no O2 or CO sensors in the kiln or occupied workspaces" (Technical Safety BC, **snippet**). [Technical Safety BC incident investigation](https://www.technicalsafetybc.ca/regulatory-resources/incident-investigations/sawmill-workers-treated-for-carbon-monoxide-exposure-from-kiln-operations)
- Gas explosion. Arizona, 1986, concrete-block steam curing kiln: one worker died in a natural-gas explosion blamed on faulty burner operation that let gas build up. Carbon build-up in the kiln and blower pointed to poor combustion **(snippet)**. [OSHA accident report 978536](https://www.osha.gov/ords/imis/accidentsearch.accident_detail?id=978536)
- CO deaths at a lime kiln: two people entered a kiln that was not isolated from a neighbouring kiln running an induced-draft fan, which forced gas in. A blank flange would have prevented it **(snippet)**. [SafetyNow/BHHC, "Fan forces gas into kiln"](https://bhhcsafetycenter.com/fan-forces-gas-into-kiln/)
- Hot, invisible gas at a peep under positive pressure can cause severe burns **(fetched)**. [Ceramics Monthly, Techno File: Kiln Atmosphere](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/Techno-File-Kiln-Atmosphere)
- Already in the repo, from Ward Burner Systems: too small a flue causes "back-pressure, safety-valve shutdowns, burned thermocouples, stalling". [Repo: docs/research/combustion-and-masonry.md §4.1, item S32](../../docs/research/combustion-and-masonry.md)
- Lid failures in kilns generally: cracking from hot-face expansion, broken banding, hinge overload (see §4 sources). [CAD thread 6663](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/6663-help-me-decide-what-to-do-about-a-cracked-kiln-lid/)

### Inferences
- A cast manifold in the lid feeding many ports is, in effect, a large ribbon burner. If it carries **premixed** gas and air, the ribbon-burner flashback mechanism applies directly, and it gets worse in a lid because heat rises into the manifold both while firing and after shutdown (chimney effect). If it carries **raw gas only** to nozzle-mix burners, flashback in the manifold is not the mechanism, but heat soak still threatens the seals, valves, regulators and hoses on the lid. I found no source on what heat soak does to raw propane in a hot manifold, so I make no claim about it.
- Turndown is when kilns are most vulnerable: during candling and early warm-up the flow per port is lowest. That is exactly when ribbon-burner sources say burnback starts. Fewer, larger ports, or zoned valves that shut whole groups of ports instead of turning them all down, are the documented forge remedies.

### Gaps
- No documented accident or forum report involving a gas kiln with burners in a lid, crown or moving hood.
- No source on the failure rates of cast-iron or cast-aluminium manifolds under kiln heat soak, or on regulator or hose failures attributable to kiln radiant heat specifically.
